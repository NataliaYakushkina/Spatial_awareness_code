# The Madonna of Port Lligat (First Version)

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Madonna of Port Lligat (first version) / La Madonna de Portlligat (primera versión)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1949
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 49.5 × 38.3 cm (19.5 × 15.1 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** P 643
- **Location:** Haggerty Museum of Art, Marquette University, Milwaukee, Wisconsin
- **Provenance:** Presented to Pope Pius XII (1949); Mr. and Mrs. Ira Haupt; gifted to Haggerty Museum (1959). Inventory no. 59.9
- **Period:** Classic / Nuclear mysticism period
- **Genre:** Religious painting

## Subject

A seated Madonna — modelled on Gala, Dalí's wife — holds the infant Christ on her lap in a classical frontal pose inspired by Piero della Francesca's Madonna and Child with Angels and Six Saints (Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan). Both the Madonna and Child have rectangular openings cut through their torsos, through which the landscape behind is visible — a revolutionary application of Dalí's emerging nuclear mysticism concept that matter is fundamentally discontinuous and permeable. The figures are set within an architectural framework of stone arches and ledges, also punctured and fragmented, floating above the serene bay of Port Lligat. Above the Madonna's head, a suspended egg hangs from a scallop shell — a direct quotation from Piero's painting, though reversed. Marine objects — sea urchins, shells, fish — reference the daily life of Port Lligat. A long nail is affixed to the left side of the altar, from which a piece of decaying meat hangs on a thread. A basket of bread sits on the lower altar ledge. Wheat and a rose are aligned vertically, creating with Christ's outstretched arms and the egg above the sign of the Cross.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Warm, Renaissance-inspired tones — golden-brown stone, rich blue sky, soft flesh colours for the figures. The landscape visible through the torso openings is the characteristic blue-grey of Port Lligat's bay. Deep red drapery recalls traditional Madonna paintings. The overall tonality is warm, luminous, and devotional.
- **Brushwork:** Meticulous, academic, Renaissance-inspired. The architectural elements and figures are rendered with careful attention to volume, light, and shadow. The marine objects and bread are painted with trompe-l'oeil precision. The technique deliberately evokes the Old Masters — particularly Piero della Francesca and the Italian Quattrocento.
- **Composition:** Strictly symmetrical and frontal — a traditional Madonna and Child arrangement within an architectural frame. The central axis runs through the egg, the Madonna's head, the Christ Child, and the basket of bread. The rectangular openings in the figures create a visual tension between solid form and empty space, presence and absence.
- **Light/Atmosphere:** Clear, even Mediterranean light illuminates the scene, consistent with both Renaissance painting conventions and the actual light at Port Lligat. The atmosphere is serene, contemplative, and sacred.

## Context

Dalí painted the Madonna of Port Lligat in 1949, the year that marked his definitive return to Catholic faith and the beginning of his nuclear mysticism period. The painting is explicitly based on Piero della Francesca's Madonna and Child with Angels and Six Saints (c. 1472), from which Dalí borrowed the suspended egg motif, the frontal symmetry, and the architectural framing.

Upon completion, Dalí presented the painting to Pope Pius XII in a private audience at the Vatican, seeking and receiving papal approval for the work. This extraordinary endorsement — the Pope blessing a Surrealist painting — validated Dalí's claim that modern art could serve Catholic devotion. The papal audience was a defining moment in Dalí's career, confirming his pivot from secular Surrealism to sacred art.

Dalí explained the "dematerialization" of the figures — the rectangular openings in their torsos — as "the equivalent in physics, in this atomic age, of divine gravitation." The fragmentation reflects both the atomic discontinuity of matter and the theological concept of transubstantiation — the transformation of bread and wine into the body and blood of Christ. The use of Gala as the model for the Madonna established a pattern that would continue throughout Dalí's religious painting.

A second, much larger version (275.3 × 209.8 cm) was completed in 1950 and is held by the Fukuoka Art Museum, Japan.

## Artistic Intent

- **Reconcile science and faith.** The painting's central innovation — the "windowed" torsos — demonstrates Dalí's conviction that atomic physics and Catholic theology describe the same reality: a world in which solid matter is permeable, transformable, and ultimately spiritual.
- **Renew the tradition of sacred art.** By reimagining a Renaissance masterpiece through the lens of nuclear physics, Dalí asserted that religious painting must evolve with scientific knowledge while remaining rooted in classical form and devotion.
- **Seek ecclesiastical validation.** The decision to present the work to Pope Pius XII reflects Dalí's serious commitment to his religious art — this was not provocation but a genuine bid for acceptance within the Catholic tradition.
- **Honour Port Lligat as sacred space.** The marine objects, the specific landscape, and the title itself elevate Port Lligat from a mere backdrop to a site of divine revelation — the place where the Madonna appears.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Foundation of Nuclear Mysticism.** This painting is widely considered the inaugural work of Dalí's nuclear mysticism period — the first major canvas to fuse Catholic iconography with atomic physics. All subsequent religious paintings (Christ of Saint John of the Cross, Corpus Hypercubus, Galatea of the Spheres) build upon its conceptual foundation.
- **Papal-endorsed Surrealism.** The Pope's approval of the work gave Dalí a unique claim: he was the only Surrealist artist to receive ecclesiastical sanction for his painting — a fact he publicized extensively.
- **Renaissance revival.** The direct quotation from Piero della Francesca established Dalí's position as an artist within the classical tradition, not merely referencing it but actively continuing it with contemporary scientific content.
- **Study and masterwork.** The small first version served as a preparatory painting for the monumental 1950 canvas, demonstrating Dalí's practice of developing compositions at intimate scale before enlarging them.
