# Portrait of Gala with Two Lamb Chops Balanced on Her Shoulder

## Metadata

- **Title:** Portrait of Gala with Two Lamb Chops Balanced on Her Shoulder (Portrait de Gala portant deux côtelettes en équilibre sur son épaule)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** c. 1933–1934
- **Medium:** Oil on wood panel
- **Dimensions:** 6.8 × 8.8 cm (2.7 × 3.5 in)
- **Location:** Dalí Theatre-Museum, Figueres, Spain (Fundació Gala-Salvador Dalí). A related study is held at the Art Institute of Chicago.
- **Period:** Surrealist period
- **Genre:** Portrait

## Subject

Gala, Dalí's wife and muse, is depicted in profile view against a characteristic Catalan Surrealist landscape. Her face is rendered with serene, almost classical precision — eyes closed or downcast, expression contemplative and enigmatic. Upon her bare shoulder, defying gravity and reason, two raw lamb chops are balanced in perfect equilibrium. The landscape behind her shows the arid, rocky terrain typical of Cap de Creus and Port Lligat, with architectural elements — possibly ruins or fragmentary walls — visible in the middle distance. The overall composition is intimate, tender, and quietly absurd.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Warm, golden tones dominate — the amber light of the Catalan coast, the warm flesh of Gala's skin, the pink-red of the raw lamb chops. The landscape is rendered in characteristic Dalínian ochres and sandy browns. The sky is a clear, warm blue. The colour scheme creates an atmosphere of Mediterranean warmth and intimacy.
- **Brushwork:** Remarkably precise given the miniature scale. Gala's features are modelled with careful attention to light and shadow, achieving a convincing portrait likeness on a surface smaller than a postcard. The lamb chops are rendered with naturalistic detail — visible fat, bone, and muscle texture. The landscape is painted with a more atmospheric, abbreviated touch appropriate to the tiny format.
- **Composition:** Simple profile portrait composition — Gala's head and shoulder fill most of the small panel, with the landscape serving as background. The two lamb chops, placed casually yet impossibly on her shoulder, are the sole Surrealist disruption of an otherwise classical portrait arrangement.
- **Scale:** Exceptionally small — at 6.8 × 8.8 cm, this is one of the tiniest paintings in Dalí's catalogue, essentially a miniature. The intimate scale suggests a private, devotional quality — a love token rather than a public artwork.

## Context

Dalí painted this miniature portrait around 1933–1934, shortly after settling in Port Lligat with Gala and during the most fertile period of his Surrealist production. In his autobiography The Secret Life of Salvador Dalí (1942), he provided a characteristically oblique explanation: "As soon as we had got settled in Portlligat I painted a portrait of Gala with a pair of raw chops poised on her shoulder. The meaning of this, as I later learned, was that instead of eating her, I had decided to eat a pair of raw chops instead. The chops were in effect the expiatory victims of abortive sacrifice — like Abraham's ram, and William Tell's apple."

The lamb chops thus function as a Surrealist displacement: Dalí's overwhelming desire for Gala — his impulse to "consume" her completely, to absorb her into himself — is redirected onto the substitute objects. This connects to Dalí's broader obsession during this period with edible, digestible, and intestinal imagery. He wrote that his "edible, intestinal and digestive representations at this period assumed an increasingly insistent character."

The painting was exhibited as early as 1936 at the Alex Reid & Lefevre gallery in London. It has been in the Fundació Gala-Salvador Dalí collection and is displayed at the Dalí Theatre-Museum in Figueres.

## Artistic Intent

- **Express Surrealist desire through displacement.** The lamb chops are substitute objects for Dalí's desire to "devour" Gala — a visual translation of Freudian displacement, where forbidden desire is redirected onto a permissible substitute.
- **Create a devotional miniature.** The tiny scale and tender execution suggest a private love object — a portrait made for intimate contemplation rather than public display.
- **Introduce the absurd into the intimate.** The juxtaposition of a classically beautiful portrait with raw meat creates the characteristic Surrealist shock of the marvellous encountered in the mundane.
- **Explore the erotics of food.** The painting belongs to Dalí's intense engagement during this period with the connections between eating, desire, and love — themes that would recur throughout his career.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Surrealist miniature.** At 6.8 × 8.8 cm, the painting is among the smallest significant works in the Surrealist canon, demonstrating that the movement's disruptions could operate at any scale.
- **Portrait as fetish object.** The tiny scale and precious execution relate the painting to the tradition of portrait miniatures — love tokens exchanged between intimates — while subverting that tradition with raw meat.
- **Dalí's food symbolism.** The lamb chops inaugurate one of Dalí's most persistent motifs: the use of food (bread, eggs, meat, beans) as a symbolic language for desire, sacrifice, and transformation. This strand would eventually lead to the Eucharistic symbolism of his nuclear mysticism period.
- **Gala as muse.** The painting is among the earliest and most intimate of the hundreds of portraits Dalí made of Gala across five decades — a founding image of what would become the central relationship in his art.
