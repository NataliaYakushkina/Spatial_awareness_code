# Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee Around a Pomegranate a Second Before Awakening

## Metadata

- **Title:** Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee Around a Pomegranate a Second Before Awakening (Sueño causado por el vuelo de una abeja alrededor de una granada un segundo antes de despertar)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1944
- **Medium:** Oil on wood panel
- **Dimensions:** 51 × 41 cm (20 × 16.1 in)
- **Location:** Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, Madrid
- **Provenance:** Private collection; Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza collection (acquired 1962); Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum (1992)
- **Period:** American period / late Surrealist
- **Genre:** Surrealist figural / dreamscape

## Subject

The nude figure of Gala, Dalí's wife and muse, floats in levitation above a flat rock that itself hovers over a calm, crystalline sea — likely a depiction of the bay at Port Lligat. Beside her sleeping body, two suspended droplets of water, a small pomegranate, and a bee hang in midair. Above her, the dream narrative erupts: from a gigantic pomegranate bursts a monstrous rockfish, from whose gaping mouth two ferocious golden tigers leap in sequence, the second lunging toward Gala with bared claws, preceded by a bayonet on a rifle that is about to pierce her outstretched arm. In the far background, a Bernini-inspired elephant with impossibly thin stilt-legs carries an obelisk across the surface of the sea. The entire scene is bathed in brilliant, clear Mediterranean light.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Predominantly cool blues and soft greys for the expansive sea and sky, creating a serene, dreamlike atmosphere. The nude figure is rendered in warm, luminous flesh tones. The pomegranates, tigers, and fish explode in vivid oranges, yellows, and warm browns — a chromatic shock against the cool background. The elephant is rendered in muted brown-grey tones.
- **Brushwork:** Hyperrealist — Dalí's characteristic "hand-painted dream photograph" technique. Every element, from the texture of tiger fur to the translucent water droplets, is painted with meticulous, almost photographic precision. The smooth, blended surfaces create an uncanny naturalism that makes the impossible seem palpable.
- **Composition:** The canvas is divided into two zones: the lower register of tranquil sleep (Gala on the rock, calm sea) and the upper register of violent dream eruption (the cascading chain of fish–tigers–bayonet). The diagonal trajectory from upper left to lower right creates a dynamic thrust toward Gala's arm, visualizing the split-second before awakening.
- **Light/Atmosphere:** Bright, even daylight with long shadows suggesting late afternoon. The clarity of the light intensifies the hyperreality — this is a dream taking place in full daylight, not in darkness.

## Context

Dalí painted this work in 1944 during his residence in the United States (1940–1948), a period in which he produced relatively few paintings amid broader commercial and artistic activities. The work marks a return to his paranoiac-critical method after a phase of more classical exploration. It was one of the few paintings he completed during his American sojourn.

In 1962, Dalí explained the painting's conceptual foundation: he had sought to put into images Freud's discovery of the "typical dream with a lengthy narrative," where an instantaneous external stimulus — a bee's buzz, a rod falling on the neck — generates an elaborate dream that culminates in awakening. The buzzing bee provokes the sensation of an impending sting, which triggers the entire cascading dream sequence and will ultimately wake Gala.

The Bernini elephant references the Elephant and Obelisk sculpture (1667) in Rome's Piazza della Minerva, which itself derived from an older literary source describing elephants in dreams. The pomegranate carries dual symbolism: it is both a classical fertility symbol associated with Venus/Persephone and a Christian emblem of resurrection. The fish may allude to Hieronymus Bosch, whose fantastical imagery Dalí admired.

The painting entered the collection of Baron Thyssen-Bornemisza and is now a centrepiece of the Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza in Madrid.

## Artistic Intent

- **Visualize Freudian dream mechanics.** Dalí aimed to depict the entire chain reaction from external stimulus (bee) through subconscious elaboration (fish, tigers, bayonet) to the threshold of awakening — all compressed into a single frozen instant.
- **Return to the paranoiac-critical method.** After exploring more classical styles in the early 1940s, Dalí reasserted his signature technique of inducing hallucinatory perceptions and translating them into hyperrealist imagery.
- **Celebrate Gala as muse and subject.** The painting is simultaneously a love portrait, a psychoanalytic illustration, and a Surrealist manifesto — with Gala, as always, at its centre.
- **Bridge Old Master technique and Surrealist content.** The Bernini reference, the Bosch-like fish, and the Renaissance-quality rendering demonstrate Dalí's insistence on classical craft in service of the irrational.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Iconic Surrealist image.** Among the most widely recognized Surrealist paintings, the work epitomizes Dalí's ability to render the irrational with photographic clarity. It has become a canonical example of how Surrealist art visualizes the unconscious.
- **Freudian dream illustration.** The painting remains the most famous visual translation of Freud's dream theory — the idea that lengthy dream narratives can be triggered by instantaneous stimuli — making it a bridge between psychoanalysis and art history.
- **Dalí's stilt-legged elephant.** This painting established the impossibly thin-legged elephant as one of Dalí's signature motifs, recurring in The Temptation of Saint Anthony (1946) and The Elephants (1948).
- **Anticipation of Pop and psychedelic art.** The painting's vivid, poster-ready imagery and dreamlike clarity anticipate the visual language of 1960s psychedelia and pop art, becoming one of the most reproduced Surrealist images of the twentieth century.
