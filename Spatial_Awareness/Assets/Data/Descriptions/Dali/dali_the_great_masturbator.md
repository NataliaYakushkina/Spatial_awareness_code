# The Great Masturbator

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Great Masturbator (El gran masturbador / Le grand masturbateur)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1929
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 110 × 150.5 cm (43.3 × 59.3 in)
- **Signature:** Signed and dated lower left corner: "Salvador Dalí 1929"
- **Location:** Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofía (MNCARS), Madrid, Spain
- **Provenance:** Artist's personal collection; after Dalí's death in 1989, willed to the Spanish national collection
- **Period:** Surrealist period (1929–1940)
- **Genre:** Surrealist / Symbolic / Autobiographical painting

## Subject

A vast, barren landscape stretches beneath a sharp blue sky, divided by a low horizon into unequal halves — an arid terrain that merges into beach in the lower portion, and a luminous sky above. Dominating the composition is an enormous, amorphous, waxy-yellow form occupying the centre of the canvas — a distorted, stylized self-portrait of Dalí in profile, eyes closed, with long eyelashes, suggesting sleep or dream. The figure's nose presses heavily against the ground. Where the mouth should be, a large grasshopper (or locust) clings to the face, its abdomen crawling with ants — Dalí's persistent symbols of putrefaction and mortality.

Emerging from the back of the central head, a nude female figure rises — widely interpreted as Gala Éluard, who would become Dalí's wife and lifelong muse. She had only recently entered his life in the summer of 1929. Her face approaches the thinly clothed groin of a male figure seen only from the waist down, with bleeding cuts on his knees, suggesting the imminence of a sexual act. To the right, a formation of two rocks and a potted dry plant are stacked in an impossible balancing act. A lion's head appears nearby, its mouth open in a snarl. In the periphery, small figures cast long shadows across the desert — one seems to walk away hurriedly into the distance. Scattered across the landscape are other biomorphic forms and faintly rendered images.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** The central head form is a warm, waxy ochre-yellow with red-pink cheeks and distinctly warm flesh tones. The landscape is sandy brown-ochre merging into pale beach tones. The sky is a clear, sharp cerulean blue. The grasshopper is rendered in naturalistic greens and browns. The nude female figure has pale flesh tones. The ants are tiny, meticulously rendered black dots. Overall, the palette contrasts the hot, organic warmth of the central form against the cool emptiness of the blue sky.
- **Brushwork:** Extremely precise and smooth, consistent with Dalí's "hand-painted dream photographs" approach. The central head form is modelled with careful tonal gradations. The insects are rendered with entomological accuracy. The distant landscape is painted with a thin, atmospheric quality reminiscent of Giorgio de Chirico's metaphysical spaces. There are almost no visible brushstrokes.
- **Composition:** The layout recalls de Chirico's compositional approach — elements scattered around a low horizon dividing the canvas, with vast empty space creating feelings of isolation and disorientation. The central head form occupies the compositional centre, but the various subsidiary elements (the couple, the lion, the walking figures, the rocks) spread across the canvas like objects in a dream, without logical spatial relationships.
- **Atmosphere:** The painting has a hot, dry, Mediterranean stillness. The sharp shadows suggest intense sunlight, yet the mood is one of anxiety and vulnerability rather than warmth. The closed eyes of the central figure create a sense of interiority — we are looking at the inside of a dream.

## Context

Dalí painted The Great Masturbator in the summer of 1929, the same year he officially joined the Surrealist movement and the same summer he met Gala Éluard (then wife of the Surrealist poet Paul Éluard) during her visit to Cadaqués with her husband and other Surrealist associates. Their meeting was transformative: Gala became his lover, muse, business manager, and eventually his wife. The painting is understood as a deeply autobiographical document of Dalí's sexual anxieties and desires at the exact moment of this pivotal encounter.

The central distorted face — which would reappear two years later as the amorphous creature in The Persistence of Memory — was modelled after a natural rock formation at Cap de Creus, the weathered promontory near Dalí's home in Port Lligat. Dalí transformed this geological feature into a recurring self-portrait that functions as his alter ego in multiple paintings.

Dalí's conflicted relationship with sexuality was profound and lifelong. In his youth, his father had deliberately left out a medical textbook with graphic photographs of advanced venereal diseases to "educate" the boy about the dangers of sex. The horrifying images fascinated and traumatized young Dalí, and he continued to associate sexuality with putrefaction and decay throughout his adult life. The ants, the grasshopper (which Dalí explicitly associated with fear and being consumed), and the bleeding knees all reference these anxieties.

The grasshopper held particular personal significance. In his childhood, Dalí had a severe phobia of grasshoppers (or locusts), which he associated with being swallowed or consumed. He recounted seeing a dead lizard covered in ants as a child, and described finding a pet wounded bat devoured by ants the next morning — formative experiences of biological horror that infuse this painting.

The painting remained in Dalí's personal possession throughout his life and was never sold. After his death in 1989, it was bequeathed to the Spanish state and is now displayed at the Museo Reina Sofía alongside Picasso's Guernica as one of the museum's centrepiece works.

## Artistic Intent

- **Create a visual autobiography of sexual anxiety.** Dalí described the painting as addressing his "anxieties, fears, and sexual obsessions" directly. Unlike many Surrealist works that cloak personal content in abstraction, The Great Masturbator confronts the viewer with its autobiographical specificity.
- **Apply the paranoiac-critical method to self-analysis.** The painting represents Dalí's use of his newly developed method to explore his own psyche — deliberately inducing hallucinatory imagery to externalize unconscious content, in alignment with Freudian psychoanalytic theory.
- **Document the arrival of Gala.** The nude female figure emerging from the back of Dalí's head literalizes the experience of Gala entering his consciousness and transforming his inner world. She is simultaneously a fantasy, a desire, and a threat.
- **Express the Surrealist engagement with Freud.** Dalí explicitly cited Freud's influence: "In the surrealist period, I wanted to create the iconography of the interior world — the world of the marvellous, of my father Freud." The painting's frank engagement with sexuality, anxiety, and the subconscious is a programmatic application of Freudian theory to visual art.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Inaugural Surrealist masterwork.** Painted in the year Dalí officially joined the Surrealist movement, The Great Masturbator is among the first fully realized statements of his mature Surrealist vision — establishing the visual vocabulary (soft forms, Cap de Creus landscapes, ants, grasshoppers, biomorphic self-portraits) that would define his most celebrated period.
- **Origin of the soft self-portrait motif.** The amorphous, waxy head form that dominates this painting became one of Dalí's most recognizable visual inventions, reappearing most famously in The Persistence of Memory (1931) and multiple other works.
- **Debt to de Chirico.** The compositional layout — scattered elements, low horizon, vast empty spaces, sharp shadows — reveals the strong influence of Giorgio de Chirico's metaphysical painting on early Dalí, a connection Dalí acknowledged.
- **Entomological symbolism consolidated.** The painting definitively establishes ants and grasshoppers as key elements of Dalí's personal symbolic language — ants as decay and mortality, grasshoppers as consumption and phobic anxiety. These symbols recur throughout his subsequent work.
- **Companion to the Dalí-Buñuel films.** The Great Masturbator was painted the same year as the release of Un Chien Andalou (1929), Dalí's collaborative film with Luis Buñuel. The painting shares the film's Freudian imagery, sexual anxiety, and entomological horror (the famous scene of ants emerging from a hand).
