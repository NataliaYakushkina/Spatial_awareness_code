# The Elephants

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Elephants (Los Elefantes / Els Elefants)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1948
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 49 × 60 cm (19.3 × 23.6 in) [also reported as 25 × 35 cm in some sources; dimensions vary in catalogues]
- **Location:** Private collection
- **Period:** Classic period (1941–1989)
- **Genre:** Surrealist / Landscape

## Subject

The painting depicts two elephants walking toward each other across a flat, barren desert landscape beneath a vast sky that transitions from deep crimson-orange at the top to pale golden-yellow near the horizon. Each elephant carries an ancient Egyptian-style obelisk on its back. The elephants have impossibly elongated, spindly, insect-like legs — described by Dalí as "multijointed, almost invisible legs of desire" — that appear far too thin and fragile to support the animals' massive bodies and their architectural burdens. One elephant raises its trunk as if in greeting; the other's trunk hangs low, as if weighed down by sorrow. At the base of the elephants, two tiny human silhouettes with long shadows stand dwarfed by the colossal creatures. A faint structure — possibly a small building — is visible between them on the ground.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** The sky dominates with a bold gradient from saturated crimson-red at the top through deep orange to golden-yellow near the horizon. The desert floor uses sandy brown and ochre tones. The elephants are rendered in muted grays and warm browns. The obelisks are pale stone-colored with subtle golden accents. The overall effect is dramatic and theatrical — almost like a sunset viewed through fever.
- **Brushwork:** Precise and controlled, though slightly softer and more atmospheric than Dalí's earlier Surrealist works. The sky gradient is smoothly blended. The elephants' bodies are modelled with academic accuracy while their legs are rendered with delicate, almost calligraphic thinness.
- **Composition:** Symmetrical and confrontational. The two elephants are positioned on either side of the canvas, walking toward a central meeting point. The vast, empty sky occupies roughly two-thirds of the picture, while the sparse desert floor emphasizes the isolation of the figures. The composition is unusually stripped-down for Dalí — unlike most of his paintings, there is minimal peripheral detail.
- **Scale contrast:** The enormous elephants against their hair-thin legs, and the tiny human silhouettes against the monumental beasts, create a powerful sense of impossible scale and surreal vertigo.

## Context

Dalí painted The Elephants in 1948 during his "Classic period," after returning from his wartime exile in the United States. The elephant on impossibly thin, elongated legs had already appeared in his work — first in Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee Around a Pomegranate a Second Before Awakening (1944) and then prominently in The Temptation of Saint Anthony (1946). In The Elephants, however, the animals become the sole focus for the first time, stripped of surrounding narrative context.

The obelisks on the elephants' backs are inspired by Gian Lorenzo Bernini's famous sculpture in Rome — the Elephant and Obelisk (1667) in Piazza della Minerva, where a marble elephant supports an ancient Egyptian obelisk. Dalí referenced Bernini's work in several communications, confirming this as a deliberate source. Bernini's elephant, however, stands firmly on its own four strong legs; Dalí's radical inversion — replacing solid legs with impossibly fragile stilts — transforms strength and stability into precariousness and anxiety.

The painting is held in a private collection and is rarely exhibited publicly, making it one of Dalí's more elusive major works.

## Artistic Intent

- **Contrast weight with fragility.** Dalí described his spider-legged elephants as embodying "the distortion in space" — the paradox of massive, heavy bodies supported on impossibly delicate structures. The image inverts our intuitive understanding of physics and stability.
- **Explore the persistence of desire under impossible burden.** The phrase "legs of desire" suggests these are not merely physical supports but metaphorical ones — desire itself is shown as thin, precarious, yet still capable of sustaining enormous loads of meaning and history (represented by the ancient obelisks).
- **Engage with the monumental tradition.** The Bernini-inspired obelisks connect Dalí's surreal world to the classical European sculptural tradition, while simultaneously distorting it. Power, religion, and empire are shown as floating burdens rather than grounded monuments.
- **Create a sense of phantom reality.** The combination of meticulous rendering and impossible anatomy produces what critics have described as a "phantom" or "spectral" quality — the elephants seem simultaneously present and impossible, real and hallucinatory.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Iconic Dalínian motif.** The spider-legged elephant became one of Dalí's most recognizable visual inventions, reproduced widely as sculpture, jewellery, and decorative art. A large elephant sculpture adorns the exterior of the Dalí Theatre-Museum in Figueres.
- **Minimalist composition for Dalí.** Unlike his typical densely populated dreamscapes, The Elephants achieves its effect through reduction and isolation. The barren landscape and empty sky focus all attention on the central paradox of the fragile elephants.
- **Transition to the Classic period.** The painting reflects Dalí's post-war shift toward more restrained, monumental compositions that engage with classical and religious themes, moving away from the densely Freudian symbolism of his earlier Surrealist period.
- **Visual legacy.** The stork-legged elephant image has become one of the most iconic symbols of Surrealism in popular culture, instantly associated with Dalí and widely parodied and referenced in design, advertising, and contemporary art.
