# Swans Reflecting Elephants

## Metadata

- **Title:** Swans Reflecting Elephants (Cygnes reflétant des éléphants / Cisnes reflejando elefantes)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1937
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 51 × 77 cm (20.1 × 30.3 in)
- **Signature:** Signed and dated lower left corner: "Gala Salvador Dali 1937"
- **Location:** Private collection
- **Provenance:** Edward James; auctioned Sotheby's London, June 30, 1976; Cavalieri Holding, Geneva; auctioned Sotheby's New York, May 9, 1995. During World War II, looted by German army and stored in the Jeu de Paume museum, Paris, in the "Salle des Martyrs" (1940–1944).
- **Period:** Surrealist / Paranoiac-critical period (1929–1940)
- **Genre:** Surrealist / Landscape / Double image

## Subject

Three white swans float on the glassy surface of a still lake in the foreground, accompanied by bare, leafless trees with gnarled, dark branches rising from the shoreline. The central optical illusion — the painting's raison d'être — is that the swans' reflections in the water do not mirror swans at all. Instead, when viewed in the water's mirror, the swans' arched necks become elephants' trunks, their feathered breasts become elephants' faces, their spread wings become elephants' ears, and the leafless trees provide the reflected elephants with long, sturdy legs. The transformation is seamless: the same forms are simultaneously swans on the shore and elephants in the reflection.

Beyond the lake, a dramatic backdrop of craggy, swirling rock formations and autumn-coloured hillsides rises under a turbulent sky. A small self-portrait figure of Dalí is visible on the left bank. The landscape evokes the rocky coasts of Catalonia, filtered through a northern European autumn palette.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** The dominant tones are blue-grey for the water, warm golden-brown and russet for the autumnal hillsides and cliffs, and cool whites for the swans and their reflections. The sky above is overcast with dramatic cloud formations in greys and muted blues. The bare tree branches are rendered in dark brown-black. The overall colour mood is autumnal and slightly melancholic.
- **Brushwork:** Meticulous and hyperrealistic in the tradition of Dalí's "hand-painted dream photographs." The swans' feathers, the bark of the trees, and the water's surface are all rendered with fine detail. The background cliffs and hills use a slightly looser, more dynamic brushwork, with swirling forms reminiscent of geological movement.
- **Composition:** Divided horizontally by the water's edge, which functions as the axis of transformation. The upper half is the "real" world — swans, trees, Catalan landscape. The lower half is the reflected world, where the same forms become elephants. The viewer's eye is forced to shuttle between the two readings, unable to hold both simultaneously. The deep recession of the landscape provides spatial context, but the main visual event occurs at the water's edge.
- **Optical illusion:** This is a classic Dalínian "double image." The transformation works because of the careful alignment of the swans' anatomy with the elephants' anatomy when reflected: necks become trunks, bodies become heads, wings become ears. The bare trees, reflected, complete the elephants' legs.

## Context

Dalí created Swans Reflecting Elephants in 1937 during his peak paranoiac-critical period — arguably his most creatively inventive decade. The painting was executed for his patron and close friend Edward James, a wealthy British poet and art collector who was one of the most important supporters of Surrealism. James subsequently used the painting as the basis for his memoir title, Swans Reflecting Elephants: My Early Years (1982).

The paranoiac-critical method, which Dalí had formally theorized in his 1935 essay "The Conquest of the Irrational," involved deliberately inducing paranoiac states to generate "spontaneous irrational understanding based upon the interpretative critical association of delirious phenomena." In practice, this meant training the eye to see multiple images within a single visual field — a process that produced the double images that became Dalí's signature in the 1930s.

In the same year (1937), Dalí also painted The Metamorphosis of Narcissus — another water-based double image exploring themes of reflection and transformation — and wrote an accompanying poem. Both works engage with the Narcissus myth's fundamental premise that a reflection can reveal a deeper or different truth than the object it mirrors.

The painting has a troubled provenance: during World War II, it was looted by the German army from occupied France and stored in the Jeu de Paume museum in Paris in the "Salle des Martyrs" (a room designated for confiscated works) from 1940 to 1944.

## Artistic Intent

- **Demonstrate the paranoiac-critical method in its purest form.** The painting is a textbook example of Dalí's double-image technique: the viewer sees swans or elephants depending on focus, but never both simultaneously. Dalí intended the instability of perception itself to be the content of the work.
- **Show that what we see depends on how we look.** Dalí used the painting to express his conviction that perception is fundamentally unstable — that every form contains the potential to be another form, and that rational certainty about visual reality is an illusion.
- **Invoke the myth of Narcissus.** The reflective water surface connects to the Narcissus story — the reflection reveals not the self but something alien and unexpected. Dalí subverts the mirror's promise of faithful reproduction: it produces elephants instead of swans.
- **Create a "lyrical yet disturbing" image.** Unlike many of Dalí's more overtly shocking or confrontational works, Swans Reflecting Elephants achieves its unsettling effect through beauty and elegance — the transformation is graceful rather than grotesque, which makes the perceptual instability all the more disorienting.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Masterpiece of the double-image technique.** Art historians consider this painting one of the finest and most elegant examples of Dalí's double-image paintings, alongside The Metamorphosis of Narcissus (1937) and Apparition of Face and Fruit Dish on a Beach (1938).
- **Northern European landscape tradition.** The autumnal palette, still water, and bare trees evoke the tradition of Northern Renaissance landscape painting — particularly the deeply recessional vistas of Patinir and Altdorfer — recontextualized through Surrealist transformation.
- **Descriptive title as unusual choice.** Dalí gave this painting a straightforward, descriptive title — "Swans Reflecting Elephants" — rather than the evocative or enigmatic titles he often used. The directness of the title focuses attention on the perceptual act rather than on narrative interpretation.
- **Companion to The Metamorphosis of Narcissus.** The two 1937 paintings form an implicit pair: both use water reflection as a transformative device, both engage with classical mythology, and both demonstrate the paranoiac-critical method at its most sophisticated.
