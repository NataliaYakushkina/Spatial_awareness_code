# Soft Construction with Boiled Beans (Premonition of Civil War)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Soft Construction with Boiled Beans (Premonition of Civil War) / Construcción blanda con judías hervidas (Premonición de la Guerra Civil)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1936
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 100 × 99 cm (39.4 × 39 in)
- **Location:** Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The Louise and Walter Arensberg Collection, 1950
- **Period:** Surrealist period
- **Genre:** Allegorical / Surrealist painting

## Subject

A monstrous, colossal humanoid creature towers over a sun-baked Catalan landscape, deliriously tearing itself apart. The body is a grotesque assemblage of dislocated limbs: arms and legs are switched around and turned upside down, with no coherent trunk connecting them. One enormous hand squeezes a breast-like protuberance while the other hand stretches the opposite leg with convulsive force. The creature's grimacing head — with taut neck muscles and clenched teeth — tilts upward in an expression that mixes ecstasy with agony. A limp, phallic shape drapes over the truncated hip, implicitly referencing putrefaction and death. Scattered across the barren foreground, boiled beans — small, pale, and mundane — litter the cracked earth. In the lower left, a tiny figure (sometimes identified as a homage to Sigmund Freud) inspects or contemplates the horror. The sky above is magnificent — a vast, luminous Catalan blue with golden cumulus clouds, creating a cruel contrast between natural beauty and human destruction.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Muted earthen tones for the creature's flesh — sickly yellows, pallid pinks, brownish decay — contrasted with a brilliant Catalan sky of luminous blue with warm golden-white clouds. The ground is dry, cracked ochre and brown. The beans are pale cream-white. The contrast between the beautiful sky and the horrific figure is deliberate and devastating.
- **Brushwork:** Hyperrealist precision applied to impossible anatomy. Every sinew, muscle, wrinkle, and nail of the monstrous creature is rendered with meticulous anatomical detail, making the impossible viscerally real. The sky and landscape are painted with atmospheric sensitivity. The combination of photographic precision and biological impossibility is quintessential Dalí.
- **Composition:** Strongly vertical — the creature dominates the nearly square canvas, rising from the barren ground toward the beautiful sky. The figure's self-destructive gesture creates powerful diagonal tensions as limbs pull in opposing directions. The tiny beans scattered at ground level introduce an absurd scale contrast.
- **Atmosphere:** A nightmarish vision under an idyllic sky. The beauty of the Catalan landscape makes the horror more disturbing, not less — suggesting that civil war is the destruction of beauty itself.

## Context

Dalí painted Soft Construction with Boiled Beans in Paris in early 1936, approximately six months before the outbreak of the Spanish Civil War (July 1936). He subsequently claimed the painting as evidence of "the prophetic power of his subconscious mind," though some historians suggest the subtitle "Premonition of Civil War" may have been added or emphasized after the war began. Regardless, the volatile climate of social and political struggle in Spain had existed for years, and Dalí had experienced it directly: in 1934, he and Gala were trapped in Barcelona during a general strike and armed uprising by Catalan separatists.

Dalí described the creature as "a vast human body breaking out into monstrous excrescences of arms and legs tearing at one another in a delirium of autostrangulation." The self-destructive nature of the figure is the painting's central metaphor: civil war is a nation consuming itself. The art historian Robert Hughes noted that the horizontal thigh was appropriated from Goya's Saturn Devouring His Son.

Asked why he included boiled beans, Dalí replied that "one could not imagine swallowing all that unconscious meat without the presence of some mealy and melancholy vegetable." The mundane, humble beans are both a Catalan dietary staple and an absurd Surrealist counterpoint to the cosmic horror.

The painting entered the Louise and Walter Arensberg Collection and was donated to the Philadelphia Museum of Art in 1950, where it remains.

## Artistic Intent

- **Embody the self-destructive horror of civil war.** The figure literally tears itself apart — the most visceral possible image of a nation destroying itself from within.
- **Achieve Surrealist prophecy.** Dalí positioned himself as a visionary whose subconscious could foresee political catastrophe, using the painting as evidence of art's prophetic capacity.
- **Combine beauty and horror.** The gorgeous Catalan sky against the grotesque creature creates an emotional dissonance that forces the viewer to confront the destruction of beauty that war entails.
- **Maintain Surrealist objectivity.** Unlike Picasso's explicitly political Guernica (1937), Dalí's painting does not take sides — it depicts civil war as collective self-destruction, a psychological rather than political phenomenon.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Dalí's most powerful political painting.** Along with Guernica, this is one of the two most significant artistic responses to the Spanish Civil War. Where Picasso's work is a public scream of outrage, Dalí's is a private nightmare of self-destruction.
- **Surrealist anatomy at its most extreme.** The biological impossibility of the figure — rendered with photographic conviction — represents the ultimate achievement of Dalí's technique of making the irrational utterly convincing.
- **Goya's legacy.** The painting explicitly continues the tradition of Goya's Saturn Devouring His Son and the Disasters of War — Spanish art's great lineage of depicting the horrors that humans inflict upon each other.
- **Forerunner to nuclear mysticism.** The painting's theme of physical disintegration — bodies breaking apart, matter becoming unstable — anticipates the atomic disintegration that would become Dalí's central subject after 1945.
