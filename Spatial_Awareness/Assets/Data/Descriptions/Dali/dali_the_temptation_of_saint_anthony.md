# The Temptation of Saint Anthony

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Temptation of Saint Anthony (La tentación de san Antonio)
- **Artist:** Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)
- **Year:** 1946
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 89.5 × 119.5 cm (35.2 × 47 in)
- **Location:** Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium (Musées Royaux des Beaux-Arts de Belgique), Brussels, Belgium
- **Provenance:** H. Robiliart, Brussels; acquired by the museum from Mrs. Anne-Marie Robiliart, 1965
- **Period:** Transition from Surrealist to Classic period
- **Genre:** Surrealist / Religious painting

## Subject

The painting depicts the Christian hermit Saint Anthony kneeling naked in a barren desert, holding aloft a crucifix in his right hand as a defensive weapon against a procession of fantastic temptations bearing down upon him from the right. Leading the procession is a rearing horse — massive, muscular, and dominating — its front legs pawing the air, symbolizing raw physical strength and voluptuousness. Behind the horse comes a succession of elephants on impossibly elongated, spindly legs, each carrying a burden of temptation on its back. The first elephant bears a golden chalice in which a nude female figure stands, representing carnal lust. The following elephants carry Venetian-style architectural structures inspired by the Palladian villas, representing the temptations of worldly grandeur, luxury, and temporal power. A fifth, more distant elephant carries a tall phallic tower. In the sky above and behind the procession, fragments of El Escorial — the vast royal palace and monastery near Madrid, symbol of temporal and spiritual authority — appear half-hidden among clouds. The ground is a parched desert, and small secondary figures and a mountain are visible on the distant horizon.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Warm desert tones — ochres, sandy yellows, and browns — dominate the landscape. The sky is a deep azure blue with dramatic grey-brown clouds. The horse is dark brown-black with powerful musculature. The elephants are grey-brown. The architectural burdens are rendered in warm stone and gold tones. The nude figure in the chalice provides a focal point of pale flesh against the golden vessel. The tiny figure of Saint Anthony is pale-skinned, naked, and dwarfed.
- **Brushwork:** Highly detailed and precise in the academic manner Dalí favoured. The horse and elephants are rendered with anatomical precision (except for the surreally elongated legs). The Palladian buildings are painted with architectural accuracy. The desert floor has a fine, dry texture. The clouds are dramatic and Baroque in their billowing forms.
- **Composition:** Strongly diagonal. The procession of temptations advances from right to left, creating a powerful sense of movement and impending threat. Saint Anthony is isolated in the lower left corner, small and vulnerable against the towering animals. The elephants diminish in size toward the distant background, creating a strong perspectival recession. The overall effect is cinematic and narrative.
- **Scale:** The contrast in scale between the tiny saint and the enormous creatures above him is the painting's dominant visual effect, expressing the overwhelming nature of temptation and the vulnerability of human resistance.

## Context

Dalí painted The Temptation of Saint Anthony in 1946 in a studio near the Colony Restaurant in New York, specifically in response to a competition organized by the David L. Loew–Albert Lewin film production company. The contest invited eleven internationally prominent artists to submit paintings of the temptation of Saint Anthony for use in the film The Private Affairs of Bel Ami, based on Guy de Maupassant's novel. Participants included Max Ernst, Paul Delvaux, Leonora Carrington, and others. This was the only art competition Dalí ever entered. He lost — the prize went to Max Ernst's version.

Despite losing the competition, Dalí's painting became far more famous than Ernst's winning entry. It is now considered one of Dalí's most significant transitional works, standing at the boundary between his earlier Freudian Surrealist phase and his later "nuclear mysticism" and religious painting. It was the first work to fully articulate Dalí's interest in the tension between earthly temptation and spiritual transcendence — a theme that would dominate his later career.

The subject of Saint Anthony's temptation has a long tradition in Western art, with notable versions by Hieronymus Bosch, Martin Schongauer, Matthias Grünewald, and Paul Cézanne. Dalí's version is distinctive in its use of the elongated-legged elephants (first introduced in Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee, 1944) and in its combination of surrealist fantasy with classical Renaissance composition.

## Artistic Intent

- **Represent the overwhelming force of temptation.** The procession of massive, impossible creatures carrying increasingly elaborate burdens of luxury literalizes the experience of being assailed by desires larger and more powerful than oneself. The tiny saint's only defence — a simple crucifix — emphasizes the frailty of spiritual will against material seduction.
- **Bridge Surrealism and religious painting.** Dalí described this work as a precursor to his later "nuclear mysticism" — it marks his first serious engagement with the spiritual dimension, combining traditional Christian iconography with his surrealist visual language.
- **Engage with art-historical tradition.** By taking on the same subject as Bosch, Grünewald, and others, Dalí placed himself within the lineage of European religious painting while radically reimagining it through a twentieth-century surrealist lens.
- **Comment on worldly power and spiritual order.** The fragments of El Escorial in the clouds and the Palladian buildings on the elephants' backs explicitly connect temptation to temporal architecture — the physical structures of political and religious authority become seductive burdens.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Pivotal transitional work.** Art historians identify this painting as the crucial bridge between Dalí's Surrealist period (1929–1940) and his Classic/nuclear mysticism period (1950s onward). The religious subject matter, the classical composition, and the engagement with spiritual themes all point forward.
- **Cinematic narrative composition.** The processional format and dramatic scale differences create a cinematic quality unusual in painting. The work reads almost as a single frame from a vast epic, consistent with its origin as a film competition entry.
- **Consolidated elephant iconography.** While the elongated-legged elephant had appeared earlier, this painting established the motif as a fully developed Dalínian symbol, here carrying specific architectural and symbolic content rather than functioning as pure surreal imagery.
- **Competition against the Surrealist canon.** The Bel Ami competition placed Dalí's vision directly alongside those of other major Surrealist and Post-Surrealist painters. That his losing entry became more famous than the winner's speaks to the enduring power of his visual imagination.
