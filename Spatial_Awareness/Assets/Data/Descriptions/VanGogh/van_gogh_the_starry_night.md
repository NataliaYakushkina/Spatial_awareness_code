# The Starry Night

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Starry Night (De sterrennacht)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (June)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 73.7 × 92.1 cm (29 × 36 1/4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F612 / JH1731
- **Location:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Acquired through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest (by exchange), 472.1941
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape (nocturne)

## Subject

A night sky over a village, viewed from an elevated vantage point. The sky dominates the upper two-thirds of the canvas, filled with swirling, spiraling forms around luminous stars, a glowing crescent moon at upper right, and a brilliant "morning star" (Venus) to the right of a towering cypress tree. Eleven stars blaze in the sky, each surrounded by radiating halos of light. Below, a village nestles in a valley, its small houses rendered with glowing yellow windows and anchored by a church steeple that rises as a vertical accent. Rolling blue hills undulate along the horizon. At left, one or two tall cypress trees rise from the foreground, their dark, flame-like forms reaching into the sky.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by chromatic blues — cobalt, ultramarine, and Prussian blue — in the sky, modulated with violets and greens. The stars and moon are brilliant yellow-gold, radiating concentric rings of lighter blue and white. The village is rendered in warm tones — yellow-orange windows against blue-grey buildings. The cypress trees are deep green-black with dark blue highlights. The overall effect is of a luminous night that is, paradoxically, more colourful than day.
- **Brushwork:** Among the most distinctive in Western art. The sky is animated by thick, rhythmic, swirling strokes that create visible spirals, waves, and concentric circles. Each star is surrounded by radiating halos of impasto. The cypress trees use vertical, flame-like strokes that echo the sky's turbulence. The village, by contrast, is rendered with more controlled, rectilinear marks. The tactile quality of the paint surface — thick, directional, almost sculptural — is inseparable from the painting's expressive power.
- **Composition:** The sky commands the composition. The cypress trees provide a vertical foreground anchor at left, connecting earth to sky. The village occupies a relatively narrow band, its horizontal calm counterbalancing the sky's turbulence. The rolling hills bridge village and sky. The arrangement is a paysage composé — painted from imagination and memory in the studio, combining observed elements (the view from his window, the morning star) with invented ones (the village, based on a separate sketch).
- **Atmosphere:** Visionary, ecstatic, and paradoxically serene. Despite the turbulence of the sky, there is an underlying order to the spirals and rhythms that creates a sense of cosmic harmony rather than chaos.

## Context

Painted around mid-June 1889, about a month after Van Gogh's arrival at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. The view is based on what he could see from the east-facing window of his room — the rolling countryside, the Alpilles mountains, and the pre-dawn sky — but the village is an imaginary addition, based on a sketch made from a hillside above Saint-Rémy. Van Gogh wrote to Theo on June 18: "I have a new study of a starry sky."

Remarkably, Van Gogh himself did not rate the painting highly. In a September 1889 letter listing paintings he was sending to Theo, he named the works he considered "a little good" — Wheatfield, Mountain, Orchard, Olive Trees, Portrait, Entrance to the Quarry — and consigned "the rest" (including The Starry Night) to the category of works that "says nothing to me." When he decided to hold back three paintings to save on postage, The Starry Night was one he did not send.

The painting was also unusual in Van Gogh's practice in that it was painted primarily from imagination rather than observation. He mentioned the painting only twice in his extensive correspondence, and then only in passing. Researchers have confirmed that Venus was indeed visible at dawn in Provence in spring 1889, making the brightest star to the right of the cypress tree an astronomically accurate detail, though the crescent moon is not astronomically correct for the actual date.

The painting entered MoMA's collection in 1941 and has become one of the most reproduced and recognizable images in Western art, inspiring Don McLean's 1971 song "Vincent (Starry, Starry Night)" and countless cultural references.

## Artistic Intent

- **Paint the night sky he had long envisioned.** Van Gogh had written about wanting to paint a starry night with cypresses as early as 1888 in Arles, fourteen months before he achieved it at Saint-Rémy.
- **Go beyond observation into imagination.** Unlike most of his work, this painting was a paysage composé — assembled from memory, sketches, and invention. Van Gogh was testing whether he could achieve emotional truth through imaginative composition rather than direct transcription.
- **Create a nocturnal complement to The Olive Trees.** Van Gogh explicitly paired the two paintings, sending both to Theo and describing them as parallel works sharing expressive principles: "exaggerations from the point of view of the general arrangement."
- **Express a cosmic spiritual vision.** The swirling sky, radiant stars, and towering cypress suggest a universe animated by forces beyond human comprehension — though Van Gogh resisted explicitly religious interpretation.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Touchstone of modern art.** Described by scholars as a "touchstone of modern art," The Starry Night is one of the most recognised paintings in the Western canon and a defining image of Post-Impressionism.
- **Paysage composé.** The painting's creation from imagination rather than direct observation represents a significant departure in Van Gogh's practice and aligns with the Symbolist principle that inner vision can produce greater truth than external observation.
- **Scientific interest.** The painting's swirling patterns have attracted analysis from physicists and mathematicians, who have noted that the turbulent flow patterns in the sky correspond closely to mathematical models of turbulence, suggesting Van Gogh intuitively captured complex fluid dynamics.
- **The artist's own ambivalence.** Van Gogh's dismissal of the painting he regarded as inferior underscores the gap between an artist's self-assessment and posterity's judgment — a cautionary note for art history.
