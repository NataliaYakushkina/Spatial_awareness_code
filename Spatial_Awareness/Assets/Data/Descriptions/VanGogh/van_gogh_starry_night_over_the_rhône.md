# Starry Night Over the Rhône

## Metadata

- **Title:** Starry Night Over the Rhône (La Nuit étoilée)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1888 (September)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 72.5 × 92 cm (28.5 × 36.2 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F474 / JH1592
- **Location:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris, France
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Nocturnal landscape

## Subject

The painting depicts a night view of the Rhône River in Arles, looking south-west from the eastern bank. The sky is filled with stars, including a rendition of the constellation Ursa Major (the Great Bear), shown as sparkling green and pink points. Below, the town of Arles lines the far riverbank, its gas lamps casting warm amber reflections that stretch across the dark water in long golden streaks. In the lower right foreground, two small figures — a couple — walk arm in arm along the near bank. Several small boats are moored nearby.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by deep blues — Prussian blue, ultramarine, and cobalt — for the sky and water. Stars are rendered as bright yellow-white points with radiating halos. Gas lamps and their reflections use warm yellows, golds, and russet tones descending to green-bronze on the water. The ground in the foreground is mauve. The town is blue and purple.
- **Brushwork:** Varied and expressive. The sky uses horizontal and vertical strokes. Stars are rendered as dots with radiating lines extending outward. Water reflections employ horizontal and diagonal strokes. The impasto is thick, especially on the stars and lamp reflections.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format divided roughly into thirds: sky, town/river, and foreground bank. The reflections on the water create a strong vertical counterpoint to the horizontal flow. The couple in the foreground provides human scale and emotional warmth.
- **Light:** Dual light sources — natural starlight and artificial gas lighting — creating complementary warm and cool illumination across the scene. The interplay between these two light types is the central visual and conceptual subject.

## Context

Van Gogh painted this work on the bank of the Rhône, a one-to-two-minute walk from the Yellow House on Place Lamartine. He had been thinking about painting night scenes for months and wrote to his brother Theo describing his intentions in detail before and after execution. The painting was completed in September 1888, the same month as Café Terrace at Night, and nine months before The Starry Night (June 1889, Saint-Rémy).

Van Gogh sent a sketch of this composition in a letter to his friend Eugène Boch on October 2, 1888. The painting was first exhibited publicly in 1889 at the Société des Artistes Indépendants in Paris, alongside his Irises. Astronomically, the constellation Ursa Major would not actually be visible looking south-west; Van Gogh likely observed it by turning north and incorporated it deliberately.

Van Gogh was driven to paint the night sky both artistically and spiritually. He wrote to Theo about having a need for religion that drew him outdoors at night to paint the stars. He also expressed the poetic idea that the stars might be accessible after death, and that traveling to one might be likened to taking a train.

## Artistic Intent

- **Paint the night without using black.** Van Gogh deliberately avoided black, instead building the darkness from a range of deep blues, proving that night has its own rich color.
- **Capture complementary color relationships in nocturnal light.** He described the painting in terms of color contrasts: aquamarine sky, royal blue water, mauve ground, yellow gas lamps, russet-gold reflections. The couple in the foreground was explicitly tied to his idea of expressing love through complementary colors.
- **Explore the challenge of plein-air nocturnal painting.** Van Gogh painted this outdoors at night under gas lighting, a technically demanding approach he had been planning for months.
- **Bridge observation and emotion.** While the scene is based on direct observation, the treatment of stars, reflections, and color is heightened for emotional effect.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Precursor to The Starry Night.** This painting directly precedes and anticipates Van Gogh's more famous The Starry Night (1889), which was painted from imagination and memory at the Saint-Rémy asylum. Comparing the two reveals the shift from observational nocturne to fully expressive vision.
- **Night painting trilogy.** Together with Café Terrace at Night (September 1888) and The Starry Night (June 1889), this work forms a trilogy of nocturnal paintings that constitute some of Van Gogh's most celebrated achievements.
- **Plein-air innovation.** Painting a complex nightscape outdoors was unusual for the period and required Van Gogh to solve unique technical problems of representing multiple light sources and deep color in low illumination.
