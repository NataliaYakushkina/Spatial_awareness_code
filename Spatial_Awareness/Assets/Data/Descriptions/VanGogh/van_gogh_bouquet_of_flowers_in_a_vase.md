# Bouquet of Flowers in a Vase

## Metadata

- **Title:** Bouquet of Flowers in a Vase
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** c. 1890
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 65.1 × 54 cm (25 5/8 × 21 1/4 in)
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** The Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg Collection, Gift of Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg, 1993, Bequest of Walter H. Annenberg, 2002
- **Period:** Auvers-sur-Oise period (1890)
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

A mixed bouquet of flowers in a white porcelain vase, arranged in a dense, rounded mass. The flowers are varied in type and color — including what appear to be roses, daisies, and other garden blooms — creating a lush, full arrangement. The vase sits centrally on a surface, with a simple background.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Rich and varied. Blues, whites, yellows, pinks, and greens feature prominently. The distinctive blues and ochres, combined with graphic brick-shaped hatching strokes, link this painting stylistically to Van Gogh's Auvers-period landscapes.
- **Brushwork:** Systematic, precise, and varied. Different areas use distinct stroke patterns — short brick-like hatchings, curved strokes following petal forms, and directional background strokes. The application is rapid but controlled, with some areas painted wet-on-wet before the background dried.
- **Composition:** Vertical format (portrait orientation). The vase is centered — a traditional, symmetrical placement that rejects the then-fashionable Japanese-influenced asymmetrical compositions Van Gogh often used elsewhere.
- **Surface:** Dense and energetically worked. The painting has a palpable sense of speed in its execution.

## Context

This still life is not mentioned in Van Gogh's surviving letters, which has puzzled scholars as to its precise place in his artistic production. The Met notes that its subject recalls the mixed bouquets of summer flowers he painted in Paris, the quasi-abstract floral wallpaper design in the La Berceuse of Arles, and the white porcelain vase in his Irises of Saint-Rémy. However, the palette and brushwork technique — especially the distinctive blues, ochres, and brick-shaped hatchings — firmly link it to the landscapes painted at Auvers-sur-Oise just prior to his death on July 29, 1890.

Van Gogh painted prolifically at Auvers, averaging roughly a painting per day during his final seventy days. Flower still lifes allowed him to work when outdoor conditions were unfavorable and to continue his lifelong exploration of color theory.

## Artistic Intent

- **Continue exploring color relationships through flower studies.** Flowers remained a consistent vehicle throughout Van Gogh's career for practicing color contrasts and harmonies.
- **Synthesize techniques from across his career.** The painting combines elements of his Paris-period flower studies, his Arles color boldness, and his Auvers-period graphic brushwork into a cohesive late work.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Late-period work.** This painting belongs to the final weeks of Van Gogh's life and demonstrates the technical mastery and graphic precision he had achieved by the end of his career.
- **Technique typical of Auvers landscapes.** The Met notes that the brushwork and color relationships in this still life are more characteristic of Van Gogh's Auvers landscapes than his earlier flower paintings, suggesting he was applying his landscape technique to a traditional still life format.
- **Scholarly enigma.** The absence of any mention in Van Gogh's extensive correspondence makes this painting an unusual case in his meticulously documented oeuvre.
