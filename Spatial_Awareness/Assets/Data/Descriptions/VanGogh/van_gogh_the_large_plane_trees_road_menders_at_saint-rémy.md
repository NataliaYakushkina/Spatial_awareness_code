# The Large Plane Trees (Road Menders at Saint-Rémy)

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Large Plane Trees (Road Menders at Saint-Rémy)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (November)
- **Medium:** Oil on fabric
- **Dimensions:** 73.4 × 91.8 cm (28 7/8 × 36 1/8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F657 / JH1860
- **Location:** Cleveland Museum of Art, Cleveland, Ohio, USA
- **Credit line:** Gift of the Hanna Fund, 1947.209
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape with figures

## Subject

A view of a street in the town of Saint-Rémy where labourers repair the pavement beneath enormous plane trees. The massive trunks of the plane trees dominate the composition, their canopies filled with yellowing autumn leaves. Between and around the trees, small figures of road workers are visible alongside heaps of sand and stones. The facades of village houses are glimpsed through the tree trunks in the background. The scene is framed by the arching canopy of leaves overhead, creating a cathedral-like enclosure of golden foliage.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by explosive autumnal yellows — golden, ochre, and lemon tones in the leaves — set against greens, blues, and earth tones of the trunks, ground, and buildings. The overall effect is intensely warm and luminous.
- **Brushwork:** Vigorous and rapid, with thick impasto. Leaves are rendered as swirling masses of short, energetic strokes. Tree trunks use bold, decisive marks. The urgency of the brushwork reflects Van Gogh's rush to capture the fleeting autumn colour.
- **Support:** Notably, this painting was executed on an unusual cloth with a pattern of small red diamonds (a checkered tablecloth-like fabric), not a standard artist's canvas. The red diamond pattern is visible in the painting's many unpainted areas, giving the surface a distinctive warm undertone.
- **Composition:** The plane trees function as vertical columns framing the scene, with the street receding into the background. The canopy overhead creates a sense of enclosure. Small human figures are subordinate to the monumental trees.

## Context

While walking through Saint-Rémy in November 1889, Van Gogh was struck by the sight of men repairing a road beneath immense plane trees. He described the scene in a letter to Theo: "The last study I did is a view of the village, where they were at work under some enormous plane trees — repairing the pavements. . . . There are heaps of sand, stones, and the gigantic trunks — the leaves yellowing and here and there you can get a glimpse of a house front and small figures." He noted, "In spite of the cold, I have gone on working outside till now, and I think it is doing me good and the work too."

The urgency of the season drove Van Gogh to use whatever material was at hand — hence the unusual checkered fabric support rather than a standard canvas. After completing this plein-air version, he painted a studio repetition on conventional canvas, now in the Phillips Collection, Washington, D.C. Conservation research has confirmed that the Cleveland painting is the first version. The studio repetition is more restrained, with the explosive yellows balanced by larger areas of cool colour.

In 2013, the two paintings were displayed together for the first time in the "Van Gogh Repetitions" exhibition at the Phillips Collection.

## Artistic Intent

- **Capture fleeting autumn colour.** The urgency of the yellowing leaves — soon to fall — drove Van Gogh to work rapidly and on unconventional material, prioritizing the moment over preparation.
- **Document the life of the town.** This is one of Van Gogh's few depictions of Saint-Rémy's built environment and working inhabitants, most of his asylum-period works being pure landscapes.
- **Create a study for a studio repetition.** Van Gogh conceived this plein-air painting as the basis for a more considered studio version, following his established practice of painting "repetitions."

## Stylistic Significance

- **Unconventional support.** The use of a patterned tablecloth as painting surface is one of the most remarkable material choices in Van Gogh's oeuvre. The red diamond pattern visible through the paint adds an unintentional decorative texture and warm undertone.
- **Repetitions practice.** The existence of both the Cleveland plein-air version and the Phillips studio repetition makes this a key case study in Van Gogh's "repetitions" method — painting a subject first from nature, then recreating it from memory in the studio.
- **Monumental trees as architecture.** The plane trees function almost as columns of a natural cathedral, anticipating the way Cézanne and later painters would treat trees as structural elements equivalent to architecture.
