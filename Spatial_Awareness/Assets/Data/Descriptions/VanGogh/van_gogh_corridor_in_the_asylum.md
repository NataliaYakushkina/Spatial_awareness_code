# Corridor in the Asylum

## Metadata

- **Title:** Corridor in the Asylum
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (September)
- **Medium:** Oil color and essence over black chalk on pink laid ("Ingres") paper
- **Dimensions:** 65.1 × 49.1 cm (25 5/8 × 19 5/16 in)
- **Object number:** 48.190.2
- **Classification:** Drawing (by the Met's classification, though it uses oil media)
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Bequest of Abby Aldrich Rockefeller, 1948
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Interior / Architectural view

## Subject

A long, arched corridor in the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, seen in sharply receding one-point perspective. The corridor is lined with arched doorways and windows on both sides, leading to a bright opening at the far end. A single small figure — possibly a fellow patient — is visible in the distance, either entering a doorway or walking along the corridor. The space is empty, echoing, and institutional.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Despite being on pink paper, the colors are rich and varied. The floor is predominantly red and red-brown. Walls and ceiling use yellows and ochres with green shadows. Arches feature turquoise and white highlights. The far end of the corridor is bright — a pool of light at the end of a dark passage. The overall effect is colorful but muted and somber.
- **Brushwork:** Short, quick, colorful strokes applied with oil paint and oil essence (thinned oil paint) over a preliminary black chalk drawing. The strokes vary in direction: some follow architectural lines, others create texture through cross-hatching. The underlying pink Ingres paper shows through in places, contributing to the warm tonality.
- **Perspective:** Dramatic one-point perspective with a strong vanishing point at the end of the corridor. The sharply receding lines create a powerful sense of depth and confinement. The repetition of arches amplifies the feeling of endless, monotonous space.
- **Atmosphere:** Hollow, haunted, and lonely. Despite the colorful palette, the empty corridor conveys isolation and institutional confinement.

## Context

Van Gogh voluntarily admitted himself to the psychiatric hospital of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in May 1889. The hospital was housed in a twelfth-century Romanesque monastery. During his twelve-month stay, he continued painting prolifically — completing over 150 canvases of the surrounding landscapes, gardens, and interior spaces. The buildings were divided into men's and women's wards, but most of the small cells looking onto the neglected garden were empty when Van Gogh was there.

This drawing was sent to his brother Theo to give him an impression of Van Gogh's surroundings. It served as a visual letter — a way to communicate his daily environment when words alone were insufficient. Van Gogh's time at the asylum was marked by recurring episodes of illness but also by periods of intense productivity and artistic clarity.

## Artistic Intent

- **Document his environment for Theo.** Van Gogh explicitly created this work to show his brother what his surroundings looked like, functioning as visual correspondence.
- **Transform an institutional interior into art.** By applying his characteristic color and brushwork to the sterile asylum corridor, Van Gogh elevated a mundane interior into a psychologically charged image.
- **Express the psychological experience of confinement.** The sharp perspective, empty space, and lone distant figure convey isolation, monotony, and the ambiguous promise of light at the end of the passage.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Mixed-media technique.** The combination of oil paint and oil essence over black chalk on pink laid paper is unusual in Van Gogh's oeuvre and classified by the Met as a drawing rather than a painting, despite the use of oil media.
- **Counterpart to The Bedroom.** Scholars have noted the stark contrast between this empty, institutional corridor and the warm, personal space of The Bedroom in Arles. Together they illustrate two very different phases of Van Gogh's life and two opposing environments.
- **Most powerful asylum interior.** The Met considers this the strongest of Van Gogh's depictions of the asylum's architecture, combining technical skill with psychological intensity.
