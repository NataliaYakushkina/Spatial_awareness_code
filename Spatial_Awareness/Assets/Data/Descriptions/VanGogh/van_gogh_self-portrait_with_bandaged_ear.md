# Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear

## Metadata

- **Title:** Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (January)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 60 × 49 cm (23.6 × 19.3 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F527
- **Location:** The Courtauld Gallery, London, UK (Samuel Courtauld Trust)
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Self-portrait

## Subject

A three-quarter-length self-portrait of Van Gogh wearing a blue-green fur cap and a green overcoat, with a thick white bandage covering his right ear (actually his left ear, mirror-reversed). Behind him on the left is a canvas on an easel with faint, barely begun marks. On the right, a Japanese woodblock print — Geishas in a Landscape by Satō Torakiyo (1870s) — is pinned to the wall. A window is partially visible. The artist gazes directly at the viewer with a still, melancholic expression.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Strong color contrasts. The green overcoat, blue fur cap, and warm background tones create complementary tensions. The face uses warm flesh tones with blue-green shadows. The background was originally bright cadmium yellow but has chemically altered to gray-brown in places.
- **Brushwork:** Long, controlled, predominantly vertical strokes. The brushwork is disciplined and organized — not the impulsive application one might expect given the circumstances. The coat texture is evoked through parallel hatching. The bandage is rendered with delicate short strokes suggesting fabric weave.
- **Composition:** Carefully structured with internal frames: the canvas on the easel, the Japanese print, and the window create rectangular subdivisions that organize the space around the central figure. These elements also carry symbolic weight.
- **Expression:** Steady, confrontational, and contemplative. The gaze is direct. The face is gaunt but composed. There is no self-pity in the expression — rather a quality of quiet determination.

## Context

Van Gogh painted this self-portrait in January 1889, approximately one week after leaving the hospital in Arles where he had been treated following the ear mutilation of December 23, 1888. On that night, after a heated argument with Gauguin, Van Gogh cut off most of his left ear. He was admitted to hospital, and Gauguin returned to Paris, ending the dream of the "studio of the south."

This painting was one of the first works Van Gogh produced after the incident, apparently believing that the act of painting would help him recover mental equilibrium. He mentioned a new self-portrait in a letter to Theo dated January 17, 1889, which is believed to refer to this painting. The fur cap was a recent practical purchase to keep the heavy bandage in place and protect against winter cold.

The Japanese print Van Gogh included is a real print he owned and had pinned up in the Yellow House. He modified its composition in the painting, shifting figures to the right to fit his own face into the frame. One scholar has noted that the heron in the print has its beak positioned as if stabbing toward the artist's ear.

At Van Gogh's death, the painting was in the possession of Julien (Père) Tanguy, though how he obtained it is unclear. Samuel Courtauld purchased it in 1928.

## Artistic Intent

- **Assert his determination to continue painting.** The barely-begun canvas on the easel behind him declares that there is more work to come. The Japanese print affirms his ongoing artistic inspirations. Together, these background elements frame a statement of creative resilience.
- **Confront the trauma directly.** Van Gogh chose to show the bandaged side of his head prominently rather than hiding it. This is a deliberate choice of honest self-examination.
- **Demonstrate controlled technique under duress.** The painting's carefully organized composition and disciplined brushwork contradict any assumption that it was produced in a state of mental crisis.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Biographical and art-historical icon.** This is among the most recognized self-portraits in Western art and the single most famous image associated with Van Gogh's biography.
- **Japanese art integration.** The inclusion of the Torakiyo print is both decorative and meaningful, tying Van Gogh's personal crisis to his broader artistic project of synthesizing Western and Japanese visual traditions.
- **Pigment degradation.** Scientific analysis has shown that the cadmium yellow background has darkened significantly, and red pigments on the easel canvas have faded, meaning the current appearance differs from Van Gogh's original color scheme.
