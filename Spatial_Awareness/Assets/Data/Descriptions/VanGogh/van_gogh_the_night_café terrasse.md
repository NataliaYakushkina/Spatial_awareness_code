# The Night Café

## Metadata

- **Title:** Le café de nuit (The Night Café)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1888 (September 5–8)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 72.4 × 92.1 cm (28 1/2 × 36 1/4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F463 / JH1575
- **Location:** Yale University Art Gallery, New Haven, Connecticut, USA
- **Credit line:** Bequest of Stephen Carlton Clark, B.A. 1903, 1961.18.34
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Interior

## Subject

The interior of the Café de la Gare on the Place Lamartine in Arles, an all-night establishment where Van Gogh rented a room. A large green billiard table dominates the centre, casting a dramatic shadow on the floor. The café owner, Joseph-Michel Ginoux, stands in white near the billiard table, facing the viewer. Five customers — described by Van Gogh as "night prowlers" — sit or slump at tables along the blood-red walls. A half-curtained doorway at the centre background leads to private quarters. Gas lamps hang from the ceiling, surrounded by radiating halos of light. A clock on the far wall reads past midnight.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Built on deliberately clashing reds and greens — Van Gogh's chosen colours for expressing "the terrible passions of humanity." The walls are saturated blood-red. The billiard table is vivid green. The ceiling and floor are rendered in acid yellows. The gas lamps create pools of lemon-yellow and pale green light. These colour combinations were chosen not for accuracy but for emotional effect — Van Gogh called them "suggestive colour" or "arbitrary colour."
- **Brushwork:** Thick and emphatic, with visible directional marks. The floor shows pronounced radiating lines of perspective converging on the back doorway. The lamplight halos are built up with concentric rings of paint. The walls use broad, saturated strokes.
- **Composition:** Strong one-point perspective, with the receding floor lines and billiard table drawing the eye to the doorway at centre back. The perspective is slightly distorted — exaggerated and vertiginous — giving the room a feeling of instability and claustrophobia. The billiard table is impossibly foreshortened. The empty chairs cast jagged shadows.
- **Atmosphere:** Oppressive, disorienting, and sinister. The clashing colours, harsh artificial light, and exaggerated perspective create a space that feels hostile rather than welcoming — deliberately so.

## Context

Van Gogh painted The Night Café over three consecutive nights in early September 1888, sleeping during the day. He described the undertaking in detail in letters to Theo, his sister Wilhelmina, and Émile Bernard. His purpose was explicitly programmatic: "I have tried to express the terrible passions of humanity by means of red and green." He elaborated: "the café is a place where one can ruin oneself, go mad, or commit a crime." He called it "one of the ugliest pictures I have done" while simultaneously recognizing it as "the equivalent, though different, of The Potato Eaters."

Van Gogh used the painting to settle his debts with Ginoux, the café proprietor. The painting was later incorporated into his planned Décoration for the Yellow House. Its provenance includes ownership by Russian collector Ivan Morozov, whose collection was nationalized by the Bolsheviks in 1918. The painting was sold by the Soviet authorities in the 1930s and acquired by Stephen Carlton Clark, who bequeathed it to Yale in 1961. Yale's ownership was challenged in court by Morozov's descendants but upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court in 2016.

## Artistic Intent

- **Express moral and emotional states through colour.** Van Gogh's stated goal was to convey psychological states — depravity, madness, potential for violence — through the deliberate clash of red and green, rather than through narrative or figure painting.
- **Pioneer "suggestive" or "arbitrary" colour.** By separating colour from its descriptive function and deploying it for emotional expression, Van Gogh was consciously advancing beyond Impressionism toward what he and his contemporaries recognized as a new approach.
- **Create a modern equivalent to The Potato Eaters.** Van Gogh saw the painting as addressing the same social concerns as his early masterwork — the condition of marginalised people — but through a radically different pictorial language.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Foundation of Expressionism.** The Night Café is one of the earliest and most explicit examples of colour used as emotional language rather than optical description. It directly influenced Matisse, the Fauves, and German Expressionism.
- **Programmatic statement of artistic theory.** Van Gogh's extensive written commentary makes this one of the best-documented paintings in terms of artistic intention, providing rare insight into a Post-Impressionist's working method and theoretical framework.
- **Exaggerated perspective as expression.** The deliberately distorted perspective — the vertiginous recession, the impossible foreshortening — anticipates Expressionist and Surrealist spatial distortion.
- **Part of the Décoration for the Yellow House.** The painting was conceived as part of Van Gogh's integrated decorative programme for his studio-home in Arles, connecting it to The Bedroom, the Sunflowers, and other key Arles works.
