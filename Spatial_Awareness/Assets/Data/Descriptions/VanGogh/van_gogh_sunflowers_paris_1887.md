# Sunflowers (Paris, 1887)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Sunflowers (Two Cut Sunflowers)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1887 (late summer)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 43.2 × 61 cm (17 × 24 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F375 / JH1329
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Rogers Fund, 1949
- **Period:** Paris period (1886–1888)
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

The painting depicts two cut sunflower heads lying flat on a surface, rather than arranged in a vase. The flowers are past their prime — gone to seed, with dried and curling petals surrounding heavy, dark seed-filled centers. There is no vase, no table, no background objects. The composition is stripped down to the flowers themselves against a simple ground.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Rich warm tones — golden yellows, ochres, oranges, and browns for the flowers, set against a background of blue and blue-green. The contrast between the warm flower heads and the cool ground creates visual vibration.
- **Brushwork:** Swirling, energetic strokes in multiple directions. The petals are rendered with thick, curving dashes of paint. The seed centers use dense clusters of small dots and dabs. The background employs short strokes of varying colors laid side by side in a manner derived from Neo-Impressionism.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format (landscape orientation). The two sunflower heads are placed close together, filling much of the canvas. They are painted life-sized. The viewpoint is from directly above or slightly angled, as if looking down at flowers laid on a table.
- **Scale:** The flowers are rendered at their actual size, giving the painting an unusually intimate, confrontational quality.

## Context

Van Gogh painted four sunflower still lifes in Paris during late summer 1887, all depicting cut flowers lying on surfaces rather than arranged in vases. This distinguishes the Paris series from the later, more famous Arles series (1888–1889), which shows sunflowers in vases. The four Paris paintings vary in number of flowers — from two to four sunflower heads.

Paul Gauguin was deeply impressed by the Paris sunflower paintings and acquired two of the smaller versions (including this one) through an exchange of artworks with Van Gogh in late 1887 or early 1888. Gauguin displayed them prominently in his Paris apartment until the mid-1890s, when he sold them to finance his voyage to the South Seas. An oil sketch for this painting exists at the Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (F377).

## Artistic Intent

- **Practice color theory through flower studies.** Van Gogh explicitly described his Parisian flower paintings as color studies, seeking oppositions of complementary hues — blue with orange, yellow with violet — to achieve maximum chromatic intensity.
- **Explore the beauty of decay.** Unlike conventional flower still lifes that depict fresh, idealized blooms, Van Gogh chose sunflowers that were past their peak, withered and heavy with seeds. This reflected his interest in all stages of natural life cycles.
- **Bridge Impressionism and his own emerging style.** The Paris sunflowers combine Neo-Impressionist dashes of color with Van Gogh's own developing thick, expressive brushwork.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Precursor to the Arles Sunflowers.** The Paris series established the sunflower as a personal motif that Van Gogh would develop more fully and famously in Arles. Gauguin's admiration for these paintings directly motivated the later series.
- **Highpoint of the Paris period.** Art historians consider the 1887 sunflower paintings among the finest and most accomplished works of Van Gogh's two years in Paris, successfully synthesizing Impressionist and Neo-Impressionist influences with his own developing vision.
- **Unconventional still life approach.** By placing cut flowers flat on a surface instead of upright in a vase, Van Gogh subverted still life conventions and created compositions that feel more like botanical studies or portraits of individual organisms than decorative arrangements.
