# Wheatfield with Crows

## Metadata

- **Title:** Wheatfield with Crows (Korenveld met kraaien)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1890 (July)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 50.5 × 103 cm (19 7/8 × 40 1/2 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F779 / JH1889
- **Location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam, Netherlands (Vincent van Gogh Foundation)
- **Period:** Auvers-sur-Oise period (1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape

## Subject

A vast wheat field under a turbulent, darkening sky, traversed by three diverging paths. A flock of black crows flies across the scene — their direction ambiguous, either approaching or receding. The central path leads from the lower foreground into the field but appears to reach no destination, while two other paths diverge to left and right. The wheat is golden-yellow and ripe. The sky, occupying the upper half, is a roiling mass of deep blue and black, pressing down upon the field.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Built on powerful complementary contrasts: deep blue-black sky against yellow-orange wheat; the red-brown of the central path intensified by green bands of grass flanking it. The crows are rendered as bold, dark marks against both sky and field. The palette is reduced and intense — no subtle tonal gradations, but stark, emotionally charged oppositions.
- **Brushwork:** Among Van Gogh's most vigorous and urgent. The wheat is rendered with thick, horizontal strokes of yellow and gold. The sky uses short, overlapping marks of blue, violet, and black that convey turbulence and weight. The crows are quick, angular dashes. The paths are scored with directional strokes. Every mark contributes to a sense of energy and emotional pressure.
- **Composition:** The elongated double-square format (approximately 1:2 ratio) is distinctive — Van Gogh used this format exclusively in his final weeks at Auvers. The three diverging paths create an unsettling spatial structure: the viewer is invited into the field but offered no clear destination. The low horizon gives the sky overwhelming dominance.
- **Atmosphere:** Brooding, elemental, yet paradoxically energizing. The painting conveys both threat and vitality — the wheat is ripe and abundant even as the sky darkens and the crows circle.

## Context

Painted around July 10, 1890, during Van Gogh's last weeks in Auvers-sur-Oise. He described it to Theo and Jo in a letter alongside two other large canvases of wheatfields painted on the elongated double-square format. He wrote that he had attempted to express "sadness, extreme loneliness," but also believed the canvases showed "what I consider healthy and fortifying about the countryside."

Despite the pervasive myth — popularized by Vincente Minnelli's 1956 biopic Lust for Life — this was not Van Gogh's final painting. Evidence from his letters indicates Wheatfield with Crows was completed around July 10, predating works such as Auvers Town Hall (July 14), Daubigny's Garden, and most likely Tree Roots, which scholars now generally accept as his actual last painting. Van Gogh shot himself on July 27, 1890.

The painting was stolen in 1991 along with 19 other Van Gogh works and was recovered quickly, though it suffered severe damage during the heist.

## Artistic Intent

- **Express the duality of the countryside.** Van Gogh explicitly articulated both sadness and fortification — the painting is not simply a cry of despair but a complex emotional statement about the coexistence of loneliness and natural vitality.
- **Use colour as emotional language.** The powerful complementary contrasts — blue against yellow, red against green — function as an emotional vocabulary, not merely a descriptive one.
- **Explore the elongated format.** The double-square canvas was a deliberate formal experiment of Van Gogh's final weeks, providing an unusually wide visual field suited to the expansive flatness of the Auvers landscape.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Iconic status and mythologization.** Despite the factual inaccuracy, the painting's association with Van Gogh's death has made it one of the most symbolically laden works in Western art. The myth of the "last painting" has become inseparable from its reception.
- **Double-square format.** The elongated canvas, used exclusively in Van Gogh's final weeks, represents a formal innovation that intensifies the panoramic, horizon-dominated quality of the composition.
- **Synthesis of Expressionism.** The painting's fusion of natural observation, emotional projection, and bold formal choices makes it a touchstone for the Expressionist movement that followed Van Gogh.
- **Companion to Wheatfield under Thunderclouds.** The two paintings form a pair — the same elongated format, the same wheatfield subjects, the same letter describing their creation — representing complementary emotional states.
