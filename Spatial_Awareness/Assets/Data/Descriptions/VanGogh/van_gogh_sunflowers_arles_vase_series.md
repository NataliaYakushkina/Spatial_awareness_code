# Sunflowers (Arles, Vase Series)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Sunflowers (Tournesols)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1888 (primary Arles versions; additional repetitions in 1889)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 92.1 × 73 cm (36.2 × 28.7 in) — dimensions vary slightly across versions
- **Location:** Multiple museums hold versions: National Gallery, London; Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam; Neue Pinakothek, Munich; Philadelphia Museum of Art; Sompo Museum of Art, Tokyo. A sixth version is in a private collection, and a seventh was destroyed in World War II.
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

Each painting in the Arles vase series depicts a bouquet of sunflowers arranged in a simple earthenware vase, set against a plain background. The number of sunflowers varies by version (ranging from three to fifteen). The flowers are shown at various stages of their life cycle — from tight buds to fully open blooms to heavy, seed-laden heads drooping under their own weight. The vase sits on a flat surface with no other objects.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by yellows — chrome yellow, cadmium yellow, ochre — with green stems and leaves, and orange or brown centers. Backgrounds vary between pale yellow, turquoise blue, and royal blue across versions. The effect is of yellow-on-yellow with strategic color accents.
- **Brushwork:** Extremely thick impasto, especially on the flower heads. Petals are built up in raised ridges of paint that create three-dimensional texture. The paint surface is sculptural, with some areas standing several millimeters above the canvas.
- **Composition:** Vertical format. The vase is centered in the lower third of the canvas. Flowers fill the upper two-thirds, radiating outward. The arrangement is informal and naturalistic rather than formal or decorative.
- **Texture:** The most tactile of Van Gogh's works. Each petal, seed, and leaf has its own directional stroke pattern, creating a surface of extraordinary physical complexity.

## Context

Van Gogh painted the Arles Sunflowers series in August 1888 as decorations for the guest bedroom in the Yellow House, specifically to welcome Paul Gauguin. He intended them as a statement of his artistic identity — Gauguin had previously admired Van Gogh's 1887 Paris sunflower still lifes, and Van Gogh wanted to impress him again. He wrote to Theo that he was painting with the enthusiasm of a Marseillais eating bouillabaisse.

Gauguin arrived in October 1888 and later painted a portrait of Van Gogh painting sunflowers. After their falling out in December 1888, Gauguin requested one of the Arles Sunflowers. Van Gogh refused, stating that Gauguin already had two of the Paris versions and should be satisfied with those. Van Gogh made repetitions of the Arles versions while at the Saint-Rémy asylum in January 1889.

## Artistic Intent

- **Decorate the Yellow House as an artist's studio.** The Sunflowers were part of a larger decorative scheme Van Gogh envisioned for the communal artist's colony he hoped to establish.
- **Explore the expressive potential of a single color.** Van Gogh deliberately restricted the palette to variations of yellow to test how far he could push tonal range within a narrow hue.
- **Capture the life cycle of a living thing.** By including buds, open flowers, and withered heads in the same arrangement, he created a compressed narrative of growth, fullness, and decay.
- **Establish a personal artistic symbol.** Van Gogh consciously associated himself with sunflowers. Gauguin called the paintings "completely Vincent."

## Stylistic Significance

- **Iconic status.** The Sunflowers are among the most recognized and reproduced paintings in the history of Western art.
- **Impasto as expression.** The extreme thickness of the paint application became a defining feature of Van Gogh's style and influenced generations of subsequent painters.
- **Yellow as identity.** Van Gogh's obsessive exploration of yellow in these paintings — and throughout his Arles period — established color as a vehicle for personal expression rather than descriptive accuracy.
- **Series methodology.** The multiple versions and repetitions demonstrate Van Gogh's working method of revisiting and refining compositions, a practice shared with Monet's serial approach but driven by different (more personal, emotional) motivations.
