# Roses (Vase of Roses)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Roses
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1890 (May)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 93 × 74 cm (36 5/8 × 29 1/8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F682 / JH1979
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** The Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg Collection, Gift of Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg, 1993, Bequest of Walter H. Annenberg, 2002
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

An abundant bouquet of roses in full bloom, overflowing from a green earthenware vase. The flowers fill the upper two-thirds of the vertical composition, with several fallen fronds and petals at the base of the vase. The background is a uniform yellow-green. The vase sits on a tabletop with a visible horizon line. The composition bursts with organic vitality — the roses crowd the canvas in exuberant profusion, petals unfurling in every direction.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Originally structured around the complementary contrast of pink roses against a yellow-green background. Due to extensive fading of fugitive red pigments, the roses now appear white or very pale rather than their intended pink. Traces of pink remain visible along the tabletop and in some petals, providing faint evidence of the original colour scheme. The background retains its fresh spring green, and the vase shows resonant yellow tones. The current appearance — white flowers against green — does not represent Van Gogh's intended colour effect.
- **Brushwork:** Undulating ribbons of paint applied in diagonal strokes animate the background, playing off the furled organic forms of flowers and leaves. The impasto is exceptionally thick — so thick that the painting could not be transported when Van Gogh left Saint-Rémy and had to be left behind to dry. The brushwork is confident and varied: tight, curving marks for the petals; broader, more sweeping strokes for the leaves; and rhythmic diagonals for the background.
- **Composition:** Vertical format, with the vase placed slightly off-centre. The same earthenware vase appears in the upright Irises (Van Gogh Museum), positioned at an identical angle. A shared horizon line links this painting to the three companion still lifes. Fallen fronds at the left base of the vase were added after the paint had partially dried, as a counterpoint to the broken stems in the companion Irises.
- **Format note:** This is the only one of the four farewell bouquets that shows visible revisions. Van Gogh explored different strategies for animating the background before settling on the final solution.

## Context

This painting belongs to the same four-painting farewell ensemble as the Met's Irises (F680), the Van Gogh Museum's upright Irises, and the National Gallery of Art's horizontal Roses (F681). All were painted in May 1890 during Van Gogh's last days at the Saint-Rémy asylum, conceived as a decorative ensemble rivaling the earlier Sunflower decoration from Arles. Van Gogh described beginning "a canvas of roses on a light green background" to Theo on May 11, 1890, and reported finishing it by May 13.

The painting's provenance includes a period of Nazi seizure: it was taken from Georg Simon Hirschland in Essen in 1939, following his emigration to the United States in 1938. It was restituted to his heirs in New York in 1950. It subsequently passed through several collectors before entering the Annenberg Collection.

Both Met paintings (Roses and Irises) were owned by the artist's mother, Anna van Gogh-Carbentus, until her death in 1907. The paint was so thick that both rose paintings were left behind when Van Gogh departed Saint-Rémy on May 16, 1890; they arrived in Auvers by late June, about a month before his death.

## Artistic Intent

- **Create a vertical complement to the horizontal Roses.** Within the four-painting series, the two roses compositions formed a pair — one vertical (Met), one horizontal (NGA Washington) — just as the two irises compositions formed a complementary pair.
- **Celebrate birth and renewal.** Van Gogh saw blossoming plants as embodiments of vitality and new life. The exuberant profusion of roses in full bloom at the moment of his departure from the asylum expressed optimism about his future.
- **Achieve a "calm, unremitting ardor."** Van Gogh described working with "calm and steady enthusiasm" and brushstrokes that "come to me and follow each other very logically," seeking controlled intensity rather than turbulence.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Largest and most revised of the farewell ensemble.** The vertical format and visible revisions distinguish this painting within the four-part series, showing Van Gogh actively problem-solving as he brought the group together.
- **Pigment fading as art-historical document.** Like its companion Irises, the painting has undergone dramatic colour change. The loss of the intended pink-against-green complementary contrast fundamentally alters the visual experience, making the Met's 2015 digital reconstructions essential for understanding the artist's intentions.
- **Culmination of Van Gogh's flower painting.** The painting represents the end of a trajectory that began with modest Paris bouquets in 1886 and progressed through the Arles Sunflowers to this ambitious, architecturally conceived ensemble.
- **Nazi-era provenance.** The painting's seizure and restitution history makes it a significant case study in the looting and recovery of modern art.
