# Tree Roots

## Metadata

- **Title:** Tree Roots (Boomwortels) / also known as Tree Roots and Trunks
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** July 1890
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 50 × 100 cm (19.7 × 39.4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 816, JH 2113
- **Location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (Vincent van Gogh Foundation)
- **Provenance:** Sent to Theo van Gogh, Paris, after July 1890; inherited by Jo van Gogh-Bonger and Vincent Willem van Gogh; Vincent van Gogh Foundation, 1962; Van Gogh Museum
- **Period:** Auvers-sur-Oise
- **Genre:** Landscape painting

## Subject

A close-up, almost abstract view of tangled tree roots, trunks, and stumps growing on a hillside, interwoven with green vegetation and exposed sandy-yellow ground. The composition is filled edge to edge with writhing organic forms — gnarled roots gripping the earth, stumps showing cross-sections of wood, fresh green shoots pushing upward. No sky, horizon, or background space is visible; the entire canvas is consumed by this dense, close-cropped view of vegetable life clinging to a slope. The effect is simultaneously chaotic and vital — an overwhelming tangle of life force.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Bright, even joyful — vivid greens of new growth, warm yellows and ochres of sandy earth, blues and purples in the shadows of trunks and roots, touches of orange and red. Despite the tangled composition, the colours are luminous and life-affirming.
- **Brushwork:** Powerful, bold strokes applied with energy and urgency. The paint is thick and the forms are rendered with broad, almost calligraphic gestures. The work has an unfinished quality — some areas are fully developed while others remain sketchier.
- **Composition:** The double-square format (50 × 100 cm) matches the other late Auvers landscapes. The composition fills the frame completely with organic forms, allowing no space for sky or distance. This close-up, all-over approach anticipates abstract painting.

## Context

Tree Roots is considered by many scholars to be Van Gogh's very last painting, created on the morning of July 27, 1890 — the day he was fatally shot. Andries Bonger, Theo's brother-in-law, described it in a letter: "The morning before his death, he had painted a sous-bois [forest scene], full of sun and life."

In 2020, Dr. Wouter van der Veen, scientific director of the Institut Van Gogh, identified the exact location where the painting was made — on the rue Daubigny in Auvers-sur-Oise, just 150 metres from the Auberge Ravoux where Van Gogh lodged. Remarkably, the largest tree trunk from the motif is still present and recognisable at the site.

The painting connects to Van Gogh's earliest artistic interests: in 1882 in The Hague, he had made a study of tree roots (Study of a Tree, F933), which he described as expressing "something of life's struggle." Whether the same thoughts informed the 1890 painting remains unknown — the letters give no specific hint, and the colours seem too bright for somber interpretation.

The painting was stolen from the Van Gogh Museum on April 14, 1991, along with 19 other works, but was successfully recovered.

## Artistic Intent

- **Capture the vital force of nature.** The tangled roots and vigorous new growth represent nature's relentless energy — life gripping the earth with extraordinary tenacity.
- **Explore close-up composition.** By eliminating sky, horizon, and context, Van Gogh forces the viewer into an intimate, immersive encounter with organic form.
- **Continue a lifelong motif.** The return to tree roots — a subject from 1882 — creates a poignant echo across Van Gogh's entire career, from his earliest artistic experiments to his final brushstrokes.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Possibly Van Gogh's last painting.** If the identification is correct, Tree Roots holds unique significance as the artist's final creative act — a burst of colour and life on the day of his death.
- **Proto-abstraction.** The all-over composition, the elimination of conventional pictorial space, and the emphasis on colour and form over representational clarity anticipate abstract art by decades.
- **The double-square series.** The painting completes the remarkable group of elongated canvases from Van Gogh's final weeks, alongside the wheatfield paintings.
- **Site identified.** The 2020 discovery of the exact painting location adds a documentary dimension unique among Van Gogh's works.
