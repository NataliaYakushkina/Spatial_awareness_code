# Two Cut Sunflowers

## Metadata

- **Title:** Two Cut Sunflowers (Zwei abgeschnittene Sonnenblumen) / also known as Two Cut Sunflowers, One Upside Down
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** August–September 1887
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 43 × 61 cm (16.9 × 24 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 375, JH 1329
- **Location:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (Rogers Fund, 1949)
- **Provenance:** With the artist in Paris; Paul Gauguin (exchanged with Van Gogh); sold by Gauguin in the mid-1890s to fund his South Seas trip; Thannhauser Gallery; Metropolitan Museum of Art, acquired with Rogers Fund, 1949
- **Period:** Paris
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

Two large sunflower heads, cut from their stems, lie on a flat surface. One flower faces upward, displaying its golden-yellow petals radiating outward from a dark, seed-heavy centre. The other is turned upside down, its petals curling and browning at the edges, revealing the back of the flower head. Both sunflowers are past their prime — they have gone to seed and are beginning to wither. The background is rendered in swirling strokes of blue and green. The composition is horizontal, with the two massive flower heads dominating the canvas, their textures and states of decay studied with unflinching attention.

The painting is signed and dated at lower left: "Vincent 87."

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Earthy, muted yellows and golds for the petals, contrasted with dark browns and blacks in the seed centres. The background is composed of cool blues and greens applied in thick, swirling strokes. The overall colour scheme is more restrained than the explosive pure yellows of Van Gogh's later Arles Sunflowers — reflecting the transitional moment between his dark Dutch palette and the vibrant Provençal colours to come.
- **Brushwork:** Thick, heavily textured impasto throughout. The petals are rendered with short, directional strokes that follow their curving forms. The background vibrates with energetic, almost agitated marks of blue and green. The contrast between the static, lifeless flowers and the dynamic background creates a striking visual tension.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format with the two sunflower heads placed side by side, filling most of the canvas. The close-up, tabletop arrangement — flowers lying flat without a vase — is characteristic of the Paris-period sunflower paintings and distinguishes them from the later upright, vase-bound Arles versions.
- **Texture:** The thick paint application gives the sunflower heads an almost sculptural, three-dimensional quality, emphasising the physical texture of the dried petals and seed clusters.

## Context

Two Cut Sunflowers belongs to a group of four still-life paintings of cut sunflowers that Van Gogh created in the late summer of 1887 during his Paris period. Unlike the celebrated Arles Sunflowers of 1888–1889, which depict vibrant bouquets in vases, these earlier Paris versions show flowers cut from the stem and lying flat on a surface, frequently in various states of wilting and decay. Van Gogh's interest in sunflowers was kindled by Japanese woodblock prints, where the flower appeared as a decorative motif.

The four Paris sunflower paintings are: this work (F375, Metropolitan Museum); Two Cut Sunflowers (F376, Museum of Fine Arts, Bern — formerly owned by Gauguin); Two Cut Sunflowers (F377, Van Gogh Museum — a preparatory study); and Four Sunflowers Gone to Seed (F452, Kröller-Müller Museum). Together they form Van Gogh's first exploration of the motif that would become his most iconic subject.

This painting was exchanged with Paul Gauguin during their friendship in Paris. Gauguin valued the sunflower paintings highly, but eventually sold both of the versions he owned (F375 and F376) in the mid-1890s to fund his voyage to the South Seas. Van Gogh's early Paris sunflower works represent a deliberate shift from the dark, earthy palette of his Dutch period toward the brighter, more varied hues he encountered through Impressionism and Neo-Impressionism in Paris.

## Artistic Intent

- **Study the cycle of life and decay.** Unlike the vibrant blooms of the later Arles series, the Paris sunflowers focus on flowers past their prime — going to seed, wilting, drying. Van Gogh was fascinated not only by beauty in full bloom but also by the end of the life cycle and the textures of organic decomposition.
- **Experiment with colour and technique.** The painting marks Van Gogh's transition from his sombre Dutch palette toward Impressionist brightness. The colours are warmer and more varied than his earlier work, yet still earthier and more subdued than the intense yellows of Arles.
- **Explore the sunflower as a motif.** These Paris studies represent the initial investigation of a subject that would become Van Gogh's signature. By focusing on cut flowers rather than vase arrangements, he approached the sunflower as a natural specimen to be studied rather than a decorative object.
- **Engage with Japanese aesthetics.** Van Gogh's interest in sunflowers was partly inspired by their appearance in Japanese prints, which he was avidly collecting and studying in Paris.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Origin of Van Gogh's most iconic motif.** The four Paris sunflower paintings of 1887 are the genesis of the series that would become the most recognisable symbol of Van Gogh's art. Without these experimental studies, the celebrated Arles Sunflowers might never have been conceived.
- **Transitional palette.** The painting captures Van Gogh's palette in transformation — no longer the dark earth tones of the Netherlands, not yet the blazing chromes of Arles, but an intermediate stage of muted warmth enriched by Parisian influences.
- **Gauguin provenance.** The painting's passage through Gauguin's collection — exchanged in friendship, sold to fund adventure — connects it directly to one of art history's most famous and fraught relationships.
- **Vanitas tradition reimagined.** By depicting cut flowers in decline, Van Gogh works within the Dutch vanitas tradition of his cultural heritage while applying contemporary Post-Impressionist technique — a bridge between seventeenth-century Dutch still life and twentieth-century expressionism.
