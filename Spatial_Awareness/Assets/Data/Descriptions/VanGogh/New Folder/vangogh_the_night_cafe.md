# The Night Café

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Night Café (Das Nachtcafé / Le Café de nuit). Title inscribed by the artist lower right beneath the signature.
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** September 1888 (painted over three consecutive nights, early September)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas (industrial primed canvas, French standard size 30)
- **Dimensions:** 72.4 × 92.1 cm (28.5 × 36.3 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 463, JH 1575
- **Location:** Yale University Art Gallery, New Haven, Connecticut (Bequest of Stephen Carlton Clark, B.A. 1903, 1961)
- **Provenance:** The artist, Arles; given to settle debts with Joseph-Michel Ginoux, proprietor of the Café de la Gare; sent to Theo van Gogh, Paris, May 1889; Jo van Gogh-Bonger, 1891; sold to Galerie Bernheim-Jeune, Paris, April 1907; sold through Nikolai Riabouchinsky, Moscow, to Ivan Abramovich Morozov, Moscow, c. May 1908; nationalized by the Russian Soviet government, December 1918; State Museum of Modern Western Art, Moscow, 1919; transferred (probably via Galerie Matthiesen, Berlin) and sold through M. Knoedler & Co., New York, to Stephen Carlton Clark, May 1933; bequeathed to Yale University Art Gallery, 1961
- **Period:** Arles
- **Genre:** Interior scene / Genre painting

## Subject

The interior of the Café de la Gare, an all-night establishment at 30 Place Lamartine in Arles, run by Joseph-Michel Ginoux and his wife Marie. The room is seen from a slightly elevated viewpoint, looking across the floor toward a half-curtained doorway in the centre background that leads to more private quarters. A large green billiard table dominates the centre of the composition, its vivid green felt standing out sharply against the blood-red walls and the deep yellow floorboards. Four lemon-yellow gas lamps hang from the ceiling, radiating orange and green halos of light. The café's proprietor — Ginoux — stands in a pale coat beside the billiard table, the only figure facing the viewer directly. Five customers are slumped at tables along the walls to the left and right, isolated figures hunched in postures of exhaustion or intoxication. Empty glasses and bottles stand on the tables. A bouquet of flowers sits incongruously on one table. The floor stretches toward the viewer in an exaggerated, tilting perspective, its boards rendered in thick streaks of yellow and orange. The overall atmosphere is one of oppressive heat, seediness, and moral decay — a nocturnal refuge for those with nowhere else to go.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** The painting is built upon deliberately clashing complementary colours: blood-red walls against malachite-green ceiling and billiard table; lemon-yellow lamps against deep blue-violet doors and window; orange and green halos radiating from the gas lamps. The floor is saturated yellow-orange. The colour scheme is not descriptive but expressive — Van Gogh chose these violent chromatic oppositions to evoke psychological states of unease, restlessness, and moral danger. He wrote to Theo: "I have tried to express the terrible passions of humanity by means of red and green."
- **Brushwork:** Thick, heavily loaded, and agitated. The paint is applied in dense patches and streaks — the floor in long directional strokes converging toward the back wall, the walls in broad areas of saturated red, the billiard table in thick impasto green. The gas lamp halos are rendered with concentric rings of orange, yellow, and green that radiate outward with hallucinatory intensity. The thick texture contributes to a sense of waviness and instability — table tops and surfaces appear to undulate.
- **Composition:** A steeply raked, almost vertiginous perspective drives the eye from the foreground floor deep into the room, converging on the doorway at the centre back. The billiard table sits slightly off-centre, its mass anchoring the composition. The walls recede sharply, and the floor tilts upward toward the viewer in a way that defies conventional perspective — creating a claustrophobic, disorienting spatial effect. The figures are pushed to the margins, isolated at the edges of the room.
- **Light:** The four gas lamps are the sole light sources, and their radiant halos dominate the upper half of the painting. The light they cast transforms the space — the proprietor's white coat becomes lemon-yellow and luminous green; the red walls glow with a feverish, infernal warmth. Van Gogh described the effect as resembling "a devil's furnace."

## Context

Van Gogh painted The Night Café in the first days of September 1888, sitting up for three consecutive nights and sleeping during the day. The Café de la Gare was not merely a subject — it was the place where Van Gogh had rented a room before moving into the Yellow House. He took his meals there and knew its nocturnal character intimately. The café was an all-night establishment — what was called in Arles a "café de nuit" — staying open through the night for those who had no money for lodging or were too drunk to be taken in elsewhere.

Van Gogh described his intentions with extraordinary clarity in his letters to Theo. On September 8, 1888, he wrote: "I have tried to express the idea that the café is a place where one can ruin oneself, go mad, or commit a crime." He continued: "I have tried to express the terrible passions of humanity by means of red and green. The room is blood red and dark yellow with a green billiard table in the middle; there are four lemon-yellow lamps with a glow of orange and green. Everywhere there is a clash and contrast of the most alien reds and greens."

Van Gogh called the painting "one of the ugliest pictures I have done" — not as a criticism but as an acknowledgement that beauty was not the purpose. He also described it as "the equivalent, though different, of The Potato Eaters," connecting it to his earlier masterwork through its concern for people in difficult circumstances and its use of lamplight.

The painting was created at almost the same time as Café Terrace at Night, which depicts a different, larger café on the Place du Forum. The two paintings form a complementary pair: the Terrace is warm, inviting, and open to the starlit sky; the Night Café is claustrophobic, oppressive, and enclosed. Together they present Van Gogh's dual vision of nocturnal social life — its beauty and its darkness.

Shortly after Paul Gauguin's arrival in Arles in October 1888, he painted the same café interior as the background for his portrait of Madame Ginoux (Night Café at Arles, now in the Pushkin Museum). Where Van Gogh's version conveys isolation and moral danger, Gauguin's mingles isolation with social energy.

Van Gogh used the painting to settle his debts with Ginoux. It subsequently passed through his brother Theo, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, the Galerie Bernheim-Jeune, and into the collection of the Russian industrialist Ivan Morozov in Moscow. Nationalized after the Russian Revolution of 1917, the painting was sold by Soviet authorities in the 1930s for foreign currency and acquired by Stephen Carlton Clark, who bequeathed it to Yale University in 1961. A lawsuit by Morozov's purported great-grandson, Pierre Konowaloff, challenging Yale's ownership was rejected by the U.S. Supreme Court in 2016.

## Artistic Intent

- **Express moral and psychological danger through colour.** Van Gogh's central aim was to use colour not to describe the appearance of the café but to convey its moral atmosphere — the desperation, isolation, and potential for self-destruction that pervaded the all-night establishment. The clashing reds and greens were chosen specifically for their ability to produce unease.
- **Pioneer "suggestive" or "arbitrary" colour.** Van Gogh used the painting to develop what he called "suggestive colour" — colour chosen for its emotional and symbolic impact rather than its correspondence to visual reality. This represented a decisive break with Impressionism's commitment to optical truth.
- **Capture the nocturnal experience from within.** By painting through three consecutive nights inside the café, Van Gogh sought to achieve the same immediacy he demanded in his plein air landscapes — direct immersion in the subject's atmosphere, light, and feeling.
- **Depict the human condition without sentimentality.** The slumped, isolated figures — the "little sleeping hooligans" — are presented without condescension or moralising. Van Gogh's empathy for the dispossessed connects this painting to The Potato Eaters and to his lifelong engagement with human suffering.
- **Create deliberate ugliness as expression.** By calling the painting one of his ugliest, Van Gogh acknowledged that he was pursuing a kind of truth that required the abandonment of conventional beauty — anticipating Expressionism's willingness to distort for emotional effect.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Foundation of Expressionism.** The Night Café is widely regarded as one of the most important precursors of Expressionism. Van Gogh's use of colour to express psychological states rather than to describe visual appearances — his "arbitrary colour" — would become a defining principle of twentieth-century art, influencing the Fauves, the German Expressionists, and subsequent movements.
- **Radical use of complementary colour discord.** While Van Gogh used complementary colours elsewhere to create vibrancy and beauty (as in Café Terrace at Night), here he deploys the same principle to produce discomfort and menace — demonstrating the full expressive range of colour theory.
- **Distorted perspective as emotion.** The exaggerated, tilting floor and compressed spatial recession create a claustrophobic, psychologically charged interior that breaks with the optical fidelity of Impressionism. The space is not seen — it is felt.
- **Companion to Café Terrace at Night.** The two café paintings form a diptych of nocturnal experience: one exterior and inviting under the stars, the other interior and oppressive under gas lamps. Together they represent Van Gogh's most complete exploration of night, light, colour, and the human condition.
- **Key work in Van Gogh's Arles Décoration.** Van Gogh incorporated the painting into his planned decoration for the Yellow House — the ambitious decorative scheme he hoped would furnish the artists' colony he dreamed of establishing with Gauguin.
- **Turbulent provenance.** The painting's journey — from a Provençal café debt settlement through Russian revolutionary seizure to American courtroom battles — makes it one of the most dramatic provenance histories in art, reflecting the political upheavals of the twentieth century.
