# Seascape near Les Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer

## Metadata

- **Title:** Seascape near Les Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer (Zeegezicht bij Les Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** June 1888
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 50.5 × 64.3 cm (19.9 × 25.3 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 415, JH 1452
- **Location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (Vincent van Gogh Foundation)
- **Provenance:** Given by the artist to Paul Eugène Milliet, mid-August 1888, to deliver to Theo van Gogh, Paris; inherited through the Van Gogh family; Vincent van Gogh Foundation, 1962; Van Gogh Museum
- **Period:** Arles
- **Genre:** Seascape / Marine painting

## Subject

The Mediterranean Sea near the fishing village of Les Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, with small sailing boats visible on the choppy waters. The composition is divided roughly into two zones: the animated, turbulent sea below and a sky above with scattered white clouds. Waves roll toward the viewer in rhythmic patterns of blue, green, white, and yellow. Several boats with coloured sails — white, orange, red — dot the middle distance, lending scale to the vast expanse of water. The scene conveys the restless energy of the open sea under a windy, partly cloudy sky.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Brilliant blues and greens dominate the water, with white caps and touches of yellow capturing the play of light through the waves. The sky is lighter blue with white and grey clouds. The boats provide accents of warm colour (orange, red sails). Van Gogh's bright red signature in the foreground was intentionally placed as "a red note in the green."
- **Brushwork:** Bold, rapid, expressive strokes applied with both brush and palette knife. The palette knife was used to smear unmixed paint, creating impasto effects that add physical texture and three-dimensionality to the waves. Grains of sand have been found embedded in the paint layers, confirming that the painting was executed en plein air on the beach.
- **Composition:** Horizontal, with the sea occupying approximately two-thirds of the canvas. The boats are placed at varying distances, creating depth across the water's surface.

## Context

In early June 1888, Van Gogh made a trip from Arles to the fishing village of Les Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, approximately 38 km to the south on the Mediterranean coast. He was captivated by the sea's colours and wrote to Theo: "The Mediterranean has a colour like mackerel, in other words, changing — you don't always know if it's green or purple — you don't always know if it's blue — because a second later, its changing reflection has taken on a pink or grey hue."

Van Gogh painted this seascape directly on the beach, as confirmed by the sand grains embedded in the paint. The visit to Saintes-Maries produced several paintings and drawings of the sea, the beach, and the village's distinctive whitewashed buildings. The experience of the Mediterranean — its colour, light, and vastness — was a revelation for the Dutch artist raised on the grey North Sea.

## Artistic Intent

- **Capture the changing colours of the Mediterranean.** Van Gogh was fascinated by the sea's mercurial quality and sought to convey its shifting palette with bold, contrasting colours.
- **Paint en plein air at the sea.** Working directly on the beach, with sand blowing into his paint, represented Van Gogh's commitment to immediate, physical engagement with his subject.
- **Express the energy of nature.** The vigorous brushwork and palette knife technique translate the movement of waves and wind into paint.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Van Gogh's most significant marine painting.** While he painted several seascapes, this is the most accomplished and best-known, capturing the Mediterranean with characteristic intensity.
- **Technical innovation.** The combination of brush and palette knife, together with the thick impasto, anticipates the textural experimentation of twentieth-century painting.
- **En plein air evidence.** The sand grains in the paint provide material evidence of outdoor painting practice in the late nineteenth century.
