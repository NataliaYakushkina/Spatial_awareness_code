# Self-Portrait (Saint-Rémy-de-Provence)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Self-Portrait (Portrait de l'artiste)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** September 1889
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 65 × 54.2 cm (25.6 × 21.3 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 627, JH 1770
- **Location:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Provenance:** With the artist to Auvers-sur-Oise; shown to Dr. Paul Gachet; in the Gachet family collection; donated by Paul and Marguerite Gachet to the French state, 1949 (accepted for the Musée du Jeu de Paume, transferred to Musée d'Orsay)
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy-de-Provence
- **Genre:** Self-portrait

## Subject

A head-and-shoulders self-portrait of Van Gogh, seen from the left — the side with his intact ear (the mutilated right ear is hidden from view, as in all Saint-Rémy self-portraits, which were painted using a mirror). The artist wears a formal suit rather than his usual working pea jacket, lending the portrait an unusual dignity. His face is lean and angular, with prominent cheekbones and a sharp nose. The eyes are intensely focused, green-rimmed, and project an unsettling combination of determination and anxiety. His red-orange hair and beard are rendered in undulating, flame-like strokes. The background swirls with hallucinatory arabesques of pale blue-green and turquoise — a turbulent pattern that echoes and amplifies the movement of the hair and beard.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by complementary contrasts: the fiery orange-red of the hair and beard against the cool absinth green and pale turquoise of the background and suit. The flesh tones are pale, almost ashen, with green shadows. The overall colour harmony is green-blue-orange — intense yet unsettling.
- **Brushwork:** Characteristically energetic and directional. The background swirls in rhythmic, wave-like curves that flow outward from the head. The hair and beard are rendered in short, sinuous strokes of orange and red. The suit is painted in cooler, more restrained strokes. Every surface vibrates with movement.
- **Composition:** Classically simple — the figure is centred, slightly off-axis, against the swirling background. The face is the still centre of the composition, while the background and hair create concentric zones of movement radiating outward.
- **Expression:** The face is immobile and taut, projecting stern self-scrutiny. The contrast between the fixed gaze and the swirling surroundings creates a powerful tension between inner control and external chaos.

## Context

Van Gogh painted this self-portrait in September 1889, during his voluntary confinement at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. It is considered by many art historians to be his final self-portrait — painted shortly before he left Saint-Rémy for Auvers-sur-Oise in May 1890. He produced no self-portraits in Auvers during the last weeks of his life.

Van Gogh wrote to his sister Willemien: "I am looking for a deeper likeness than that obtained by a photographer." And to Theo: "People say, and I am willing to believe it, that it is hard to know yourself. But it is not easy to paint yourself, either." He took the painting with him to Auvers, where he showed it to Dr. Paul Gachet, who described it as "absolutely fanatical."

The painting entered the Gachet family collection and was donated to the French state in 1949 by Paul and Marguerite Gachet, the children of Dr. Gachet. The Gachet donation — which included the Portrait of Dr. Gachet and other major works — is one of the most important gifts in the history of French national museums.

## Artistic Intent

- **Achieve psychological depth beyond photography.** Van Gogh explicitly sought to capture an inner truth that the camera could not — the self as felt, not merely as seen.
- **Express the duality of control and chaos.** The still, controlled face set against the turbulent background enacts the fundamental tension of Van Gogh's life at Saint-Rémy — lucidity surrounded by mental storms.
- **Present himself with dignity.** The choice of a formal suit rather than working clothes suggests Van Gogh wished to portray himself as a serious, respectable figure — not as a madman in an asylum.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Possibly Van Gogh's last self-portrait.** The painting holds unique emotional weight as what may be the artist's final act of self-examination before his death.
- **Masterwork of Post-Impressionist portraiture.** The swirling background, complementary colour scheme, and directional brushwork demonstrate the full maturity of Van Gogh's portrait style at Saint-Rémy.
- **Gachet provenance.** The painting's history — from Van Gogh's hands to Dr. Gachet's, to the French nation — embodies the intimate network of personal relationships through which Van Gogh's work was preserved.
