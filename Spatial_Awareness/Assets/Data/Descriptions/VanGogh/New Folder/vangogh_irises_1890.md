# Irises

## Metadata

- **Title:** Irises (Irissen)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** May 1890
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 92.7 × 73.9 cm (36.5 × 29.1 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 680, JH 1977
- **Location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (Vincent van Gogh Foundation)
- **Provenance:** Sent to Theo van Gogh, Paris, after end of June 1890; inherited by Jo van Gogh-Bonger and Vincent Willem van Gogh; Vincent van Gogh Foundation, 1962; Van Gogh Museum
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy-de-Provence
- **Genre:** Still life / Flower painting

## Subject

A luxuriant bouquet of irises arranged in a vase, set against a uniformly yellow background. The flowers — originally purple, now faded to blue due to the degradation of red pigment — fill the upper two-thirds of the canvas, their gracefully curving stems and sword-shaped leaves creating a dynamic interplay of form. The vase sits on a surface indicated by minimal strokes. The composition is vertical, with the bouquet rising upward and spreading outward, the individual blooms distinguished by varying orientations and stages of opening. This is one of four monumental still lifes — two of irises, two of roses — that Van Gogh painted as a series on the eve of his departure from the Saint-Rémy asylum.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** The dominant contrast is between the blue-purple of the irises (originally violet) and the bright yellow background — a bold complementary pairing. The leaves and stems provide green accents. The overall effect is richly decorative and vibrant.
- **Brushwork:** Thick, heavily loaded — the paint is so thickly applied that the canvases took a month to dry and had to be left behind when Van Gogh departed Saint-Rémy. The petals are modelled with individual directional strokes that follow the curves of each bloom. The background is more evenly painted.
- **Composition:** Vertical format with the bouquet centred in the vase. The horizon line is shared with the companion paintings (Met Irises, Met Roses, NGA Roses), indicating they were conceived as an ensemble.

## Context

In May 1890, during his final days at the asylum, Van Gogh painted four large still lifes of spring flowers gathered from the asylum garden. He conceived them as a series, comparable in ambition to his Sunflower decorations from Arles. The four canvases were cut from the same roll and share a common horizon line. In this version, Van Gogh set purple irises against a yellow background to achieve maximum complementary contrast.

He wrote to Theo on May 11, 1890, describing his progress on the series. The irises were originally purple — created by mixing geranium lake red with blue — but the red pigment has faded over time, leaving the flowers blue. This colour loss significantly alters the painting's original chromatic intensity.

## Artistic Intent

- **Study complementary colour contrast.** Van Gogh viewed the painting primarily as a colour study — placing violet against yellow for maximum vibrancy.
- **Create a decorative ensemble.** The four flower paintings were conceived as a unified group, rivalling the Sunflower series.
- **Achieve a "calm, unremitting ardor."** Van Gogh sought to bring serene intensity to his final work in Provence.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Part of Van Gogh's final flower series.** The four flower still lifes represent his last major creative project at Saint-Rémy — a culminating statement on colour and decoration.
- **Document of pigment degradation.** The painting is a case study in how fugitive pigments alter an artwork over time, transforming violet to blue.
- **Bridge between Sunflowers and late mastery.** The series extends Van Gogh's ambition to create monumental flower decorations, now with greater chromatic sophistication.
