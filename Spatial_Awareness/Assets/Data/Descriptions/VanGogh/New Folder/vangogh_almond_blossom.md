# Almond Blossom

## Metadata

- **Title:** Almond Blossom (Amandelbloesem)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** February 1890
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 73.3 × 92.4 cm (28.9 × 36.4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 671, JH 1891
- **Location:** Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam (Vincent van Gogh Foundation)
- **Provenance:** Sent by the artist to Theo van Gogh, Paris, end of April 1890; inherited by Jo van Gogh-Bonger and Vincent Willem van Gogh; Vincent van Gogh Foundation, 1962; Van Gogh Museum, 1973
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy-de-Provence
- **Genre:** Flower painting / Japonism

## Subject

Large branches of an almond tree in full blossom, set against a luminous blue sky. The composition presents the branches from below, as if the viewer were lying beneath the tree looking up, with the blossoms spreading across the entire canvas and reaching beyond its borders. The flowers are rendered in delicate whites and pinks with precise, dark outlines defining each petal and bud. Smaller buds in various stages of opening dot the branches alongside fully opened five-petalled blossoms. The branches twist and curve gracefully across the picture plane, their dark lines creating a decorative network against the radiant blue background. No ground, trunk, or horizon is visible — the composition is entirely sky and blossom.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by a luminous, carefully modulated blue sky ranging from light vibrant blue to deeper ultramarine. The blossoms are white and pale pink, the branches dark brown-grey, and the buds show touches of green and red. The overall effect is serene, spring-fresh, and luminous.
- **Brushwork:** Unusually controlled and deliberate for Van Gogh. The sky is painted with careful, even strokes, and the branches and blossoms are defined with precise dark outlines — a technique borrowed directly from Japanese woodblock prints. The delicacy of execution contrasts markedly with Van Gogh's characteristically vigorous impasto in other works from this period.
- **Composition:** The branches fill the canvas in a decorative, asymmetric arrangement inspired by Japanese prints (ukiyo-e). The composition is cropped — branches extend beyond the edges on all sides, implying a larger tree beyond the frame. The flat, unmodulated blue sky eliminates depth, compressing the image into a decorative surface pattern.
- **Light:** Even, diffused — no strong directional light or shadow. The blue sky radiates luminosity throughout.

## Context

Van Gogh painted Almond Blossom in February 1890 at the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, as a gift to celebrate the birth of his nephew, Vincent Willem van Gogh, born on January 31, 1890, to his brother Theo and sister-in-law Jo. Theo wrote to Vincent: "As we told you, we'll name him after you, and I'm making the wish that he may be as determined and as courageous as you."

Van Gogh chose almond blossoms because they are among the first trees to flower in early spring in the south of France, symbolizing new life, hope, and renewal — a perfect subject for a birth gift. The painting draws heavily on Japanese art, which Van Gogh had been collecting and studying since his time in Paris. Hiroshige's Plum Garden at Kameido was a particular influence, with its close-up view of blossoming branches against a flat colour background.

Van Gogh was deeply moved by the birth. However, shortly after completing the painting, he suffered a severe mental collapse that lasted over two months, preventing him from painting during the remaining blossom season. The painting was never sold by the family. It hung in young Vincent Willem's bedroom, and the adult Vincent Willem later founded the Van Gogh Museum, where the painting remains today as one of its most beloved works.

## Artistic Intent

- **Celebrate new life.** The painting is a joyful gift marking the birth of his nephew — one of Van Gogh's rare works created for a specific personal occasion.
- **Honour Japanese art.** The flat composition, dark outlines, decorative arrangement, and close-up view are a deliberate homage to the Japanese woodblock prints Van Gogh admired.
- **Achieve serenity.** Van Gogh described Japanese art as embodying the serenity he sought in his own life and work. This painting, with its calm, contemplative beauty, is perhaps his closest achievement of that ideal.
- **Symbolise hope and renewal.** Almond blossoms, blooming before all other trees, embody the promise of spring — a metaphor for the new life of his nephew and, perhaps, his own hope for recovery.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Van Gogh's most Japanese painting.** Almond Blossom represents the fullest realization of Van Gogh's engagement with Japanese aesthetics — a synthesis of European oil technique and ukiyo-e composition that he had been developing since Paris.
- **Departure from characteristic style.** The controlled brushwork, absence of impasto, and decorative flatness distinguish this from the turbulent, expressionist works that dominate the Saint-Rémy period.
- **Most personally meaningful painting.** As the only major work created specifically as a gift to celebrate family, it holds a unique place in Van Gogh's oeuvre.
- **Enduring cultural icon.** The painting has become one of the most widely reproduced images in art history and remains the emotional centrepiece of the Van Gogh Museum.
