# Self-Portrait (Paris, 1887)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Self-Portrait
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890)
- **Year:** Spring 1887
- **Medium:** Oil on artist's board, mounted on cradled panel
- **Dimensions:** 41 × 32.5 cm (16.1 × 12.8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F 345, JH 1249
- **Location:** The Art Institute of Chicago (Joseph Winterbotham Collection)
- **Provenance:** Jo van Gogh-Bonger, Amsterdam; sold through Frankfurter Kunstverein to Leonhard Tietz, Cologne; eventually to Art Institute of Chicago
- **Period:** Paris
- **Genre:** Self-portrait

## Subject

A head-and-shoulders self-portrait of Van Gogh facing slightly right, wearing a dark jacket and painted against a vibrant, pointillist-influenced background. The artist's red-orange beard and hair are rendered in short, energetic strokes of warm colour — orange, red, and gold — that contrast vividly with the cooler green and blue tones of the background and clothing. His deep green eyes stare out with penetrating intensity. The surface of the painting dances with particles of colour — greens, blues, reds, and oranges applied in a dense mosaic of small dabs and dashes.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Rich complementary contrasts: warm orange-red (hair, beard) against cool blue-green (background, clothing). The flesh tones incorporate unexpected greens, blues, and oranges. The overall effect is vibrant and alive with chromatic energy.
- **Brushwork:** Densely dabbed, pointillist-influenced technique — short, distinct strokes of pure colour placed side by side. The method reflects Van Gogh's response to Georges Seurat's revolutionary technique in A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, which he encountered in Paris. However, where Seurat's method was scientifically systematic, Van Gogh's is emotionally expressive.
- **Composition:** Simple, frontal, bust-length. The modest scale (41 × 32.5 cm) and the use of artist's board rather than canvas suggest an intimate, experimental study.

## Context

Van Gogh left the Netherlands and arrived in Paris in early 1886 to live with his brother Theo, who was a dealer in paintings. In Paris, he was introduced to Impressionism, Neo-Impressionism, and the work of artists including Seurat, Signac, Degas, Monet, and Toulouse-Lautrec. The encounter transformed his art. Over his two-year Paris stay, Van Gogh created at least 24 self-portraits — using himself as a model because he could not afford to pay others.

This early Paris self-portrait, painted in spring 1887 on prepared artist's board, is a direct response to Seurat's pointillist technique. The Art Institute notes that what was for Seurat a method based on the cool objectivity of science became in Van Gogh's hands an intense emotional language. Van Gogh wrote to Theo: "I prefer painting people's eyes to cathedrals. However solemn and imposing the latter may be — a human soul, be it that of a poor streetwalker, is more interesting to me."

## Artistic Intent

- **Experiment with Neo-Impressionist technique.** The densely dabbed brushwork is a direct response to Seurat and Signac — Van Gogh testing and adapting pointillism for his own expressive purposes.
- **Brighten the palette.** This painting marks Van Gogh's dramatic shift from the dark, earthy palette of his Dutch period to the vibrant chromatic intensity that would define his mature style.
- **Explore self-representation.** As one of dozens of Paris self-portraits, it is part of Van Gogh's sustained investigation of his own identity through painting.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Key transitional work.** The painting captures Van Gogh in the act of absorbing and transforming Neo-Impressionist technique into his personal style — the crucial moment of his artistic evolution.
- **Pointillism made personal.** Van Gogh's adaptation of Seurat's systematic method into a vibrant, emotionally charged technique anticipates Expressionism.
- **Early Paris period document.** One of the most significant visual records of Van Gogh's first encounters with the Parisian avant-garde.
