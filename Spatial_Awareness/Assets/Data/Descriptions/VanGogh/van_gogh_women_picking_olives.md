# Women Picking Olives

## Metadata

- **Title:** Women Picking Olives
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (December)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 72.7 × 91.4 cm (28 5/8 × 36 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F655 / JH1869
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** The Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg Collection, Gift of Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg, 1995, Bequest of Walter H. Annenberg, 2002
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape with figures

## Subject

Three women pick olives among twisted olive trees in a Provençal grove. One figure uses a ladder to reach higher branches while others gather fruit from lower limbs. The trees have gnarled, bronze-toned trunks and gray-green foliage. The ground is violet in the foreground, transitioning to yellow ochre in the distance. The sky is entirely pink. The figures are small and rendered in pink tones, integrated into the landscape rather than dominating it.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Deliberately restricted to harmonizing pairs. Pink sky and pink figures are set against green foliage; violet ground contrasts with yellow ochre distance. Van Gogh described the color scheme as "only two notes, pink and green, which harmonize, neutralize each other, oppose each other."
- **Brushwork:** Rhythmic, undulating strokes that follow the contours of the twisted trees and rolling ground. The entire surface has a curving, organic quality. Strokes are medium-length, directional, and densely packed.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format. Olive trees fill the composition, their trunks and branches creating a web of curving lines. The figures are subordinate to the landscape, emphasizing the relationship between humans and nature.
- **Atmosphere:** Luminous and harmonious despite the restricted palette. The pink sky and green trees create a dreamlike, almost sacred quality.

## Context

At the end of 1889, Van Gogh painted three versions of this subject at the Saint-Rémy asylum. He described the first as a study from nature in "more colored with more solemn tones" (private collection). The second was a studio rendition in a "very discreet range" of colors (National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.). This Metropolitan Museum version, the third, is the most resolved and stylized, intended as a gift for his mother and sister. He wrote to them that he hoped the painting would be to their taste, and told Gauguin he had sent a drawing of it, which Gauguin approved.

The olive groves around the Saint-Rémy asylum were a major subject for Van Gogh during his confinement, resulting in at least fifteen paintings. He was drawn to the olive trees both for their visual beauty — the twisted forms, the silver-gray leaves — and for their spiritual associations with the Garden of Gethsemane. Van Gogh deliberately chose to paint olive pickers from observation rather than imagined biblical scenes, in pointed contrast to Gauguin and Bernard's paintings of Christ in the Garden of Olives.

## Artistic Intent

- **Embed spiritual meaning through observation rather than imagination.** Van Gogh rejected Gauguin's and Bernard's approach of painting biblical scenes from imagination, insisting that the real olive groves of Provence contained sufficient spiritual resonance without literal religious iconography.
- **Achieve harmony through restricted color.** By limiting the palette to variations of pink and green, Van Gogh created a unified, meditative atmosphere.
- **Create a gift for family.** This version was specifically made for his mother and sister, suggesting a desire to produce something more accessible and gentle than his more intense works.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Olive tree series.** This painting belongs to a major thematic group within Van Gogh's Saint-Rémy output. The olive tree paintings collectively represent his most sustained engagement with a single landscape motif.
- **Stylization vs. naturalism.** As the most refined of the three versions, this painting shows Van Gogh moving from direct observation toward a more synthesized, decorative style — a tendency that aligns with broader Symbolist and Post-Impressionist developments of the period.
- **Human-nature integration.** The small scale of the figures relative to the trees, and their color harmony with the landscape, emphasizes the connection between human labor and natural cycles.
