# Wheat Field with Cypresses

## Metadata

- **Title:** Wheat Field with Cypresses (A Wheatfield, with Cypresses)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (late June–early July)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 73.2 × 93.4 cm (28 13/16 × 36 3/4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F717 / JH1756
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Purchase, The Annenberg Foundation Gift, 1993
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape

## Subject

A panoramic Provençal landscape viewed from the grounds near the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. In the foreground, a golden wheat field ripples in the wind, interspersed with red poppies and wild flowers. To the right, a tall, dark cypress rises like a green flame, its form towering into the sky. In the middle ground, lighter green olive trees and low bushes contrast with the cypress. Beyond, rolling blue-violet hills (the Alpilles mountains) stretch across the horizon. Above, an expansive sky of swirling white clouds and patches of blue fills more than a third of the canvas.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Warm golds and ochres in the wheat field; deep greens and near-blacks in the cypress; lighter greens in the olive trees; blue-violet in the distant mountains; azure blue and white in the sky. The overall effect is one of intense Mediterranean heat and light.
- **Brushwork:** Extraordinarily thick impasto throughout. The wheat field is rendered with dense, short strokes conveying the texture of ripe grain swaying in the wind. The cypress is painted with tall, curving vertical strokes creating a flame-like, almost writhing effect. The clouds use broad, swirling, rhythmic strokes. Van Gogh described the impasto as being "like the Monticelli's."
- **Composition:** Horizontal format organized in horizontal bands — wheat, trees, mountains, sky — punctuated by the dramatic vertical thrust of the cypress, which connects earth to sky. The composition is classical in its logic despite the energetic surface treatment.
- **Movement:** Everything is in motion. The wheat sways, the cypress flames upward, the clouds roll and spiral. The entire landscape pulsates with wind and heat.

## Context

Van Gogh painted this work en plein air in late June or early July 1889, shortly after he began his voluntary stay at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in Saint-Rémy. This was completed around the same period as The Starry Night (June 1889) and represents the diurnal counterpart to that nocturnal painting. Van Gogh described it in a letter to Theo dated July 2, 1889: "I have a canvas of cypresses with some ears of wheat, some poppies, a blue sky like a piece of Scotch plaid; the former painted with a thick impasto like the Monticelli's, and the wheat field in the sun, which represents the extreme heat, very thick too."

Van Gogh regarded this painting as one of his "best" summer landscapes. He made a reed-pen drawing of it (now in the Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam) and in September 1889 painted two studio copies: one on the same scale (now in the National Gallery, London) and a smaller replica intended as a gift for his mother and sister (private collection). There are thus four versions total: the Met's plein-air original, the Amsterdam drawing, and two studio oil copies.

The Met's version was sold by Theo's widow in 1900. It passed through several European collections, eventually reaching the Bührle family in Zurich, before being donated to the Metropolitan Museum in 1993 for $57 million via the Annenberg Foundation.

## Artistic Intent

- **Elevate the cypress to a monumental subject.** Van Gogh was captivated by the cypress's form and color, comparing it to an Egyptian obelisk and calling it "a splash of black in a sunny landscape." He aspired to make the cypress as iconic in his work as the sunflower.
- **Capture the experience of extreme Provençal heat.** The thick impasto and warm palette were deliberate strategies to convey the physical sensation of a Mediterranean summer day.
- **Create a companion to his nocturnal paintings.** This landscape functions as the daylight counterpart to The Starry Night, with the cypress serving as a connecting motif between the two compositions.
- **Paint directly from nature with maximum intensity.** Van Gogh valued plein-air work for its immediacy and energy, and this painting preserves the urgency of his direct encounter with the landscape.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Part of the cypress series.** This painting is the centrepiece of Van Gogh's Saint-Rémy cypress paintings, a thematic group the Met explored in its 2023 exhibition "Van Gogh's Cypresses." The series tracks his evolving fascination with the trees from Arles through Saint-Rémy.
- **Diurnal counterpart to The Starry Night.** Scholars have noted the structural parallels between the two paintings: both use the cypress as a central vertical element bridging earth and sky, but one uses sunlit warmth and the other nocturnal cool.
- **Impasto as expression.** The sheer thickness and physicality of the paint surface make this painting a landmark of Van Gogh's late technique, where the material substance of oil paint becomes inseparable from the emotional and sensory content.
- **Multiple versions.** The existence of four versions (plein-air original, drawing, two studio copies) provides an exceptional case study in Van Gogh's working process and the relationship between direct observation and studio recollection.
