# La Berceuse (Woman Rocking a Cradle; Augustine-Alix Pellicot Roulin, 1851–1930)

## Metadata

- **Title:** La Berceuse (Woman Rocking a Cradle)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (January)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 92.7 × 73.7 cm (36 1/2 × 29 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F505 / JH1669
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** The Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg Collection, Gift of Walter H. and Leonore Annenberg, 1996, Bequest of Walter H. Annenberg, 2002
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Portrait

## Subject

A seated woman — Augustine Roulin, wife of the Arles postmaster Joseph Roulin — occupies the centre of the composition, shown from the waist up in a brown wooden armchair. She holds a rope in her hands, which extends beyond the edge of the canvas to an unseen cradle. She wears a green dress or skirt with a dark bodice. Behind her, multi-coloured French floral wallpaper — featuring stylised dahlias or similar flowers — fills the upper two-thirds of the background with a dense, pulsating pattern. The lower third of the background reproduces the red of the floor. Her expression is calm and contemplative — a symbol of maternal comfort.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by a carefully orchestrated arrangement of greens, reds, and oranges. Augustine's green skirt stands out against the red floor. The floral wallpaper provides a vibrant, decorative field of greens, pinks, oranges, and yellows. The colour scheme was deliberately designed as "a lullaby in colour" — soothing yet vivid, with graduated complementary contrasts intended to radiate tranquillity and comfort.
- **Brushwork:** Deliberately simplified and bold — Van Gogh described the style as "like a chromo," meaning intentionally popular, legible, and primitive. Compact shapes are outlined in black. Flat areas of colour are cut into decorative patterns. The wallpaper is rendered with simplified, almost schematic flower forms. This accessible style was a conscious choice to address "common folk."
- **Composition:** Vertical format, symmetrical. Augustine is centred and frontal, framed by the patterned wallpaper behind and above. The composition evokes the traditional format of religious art — specifically, the "Cloth of Honor" motif in which a Madonna is centred before a panel of rich fabric.
- **Scale and presence:** The figure dominates the canvas. The rope leading off-canvas to the unseen cradle creates a psychological extension beyond the frame.

## Context

Van Gogh began the portrait of Augustine Roulin just before his breakdown on December 23, 1888 — the incident in which he severed part of his own ear. After returning from hospital on January 7, 1889, he immediately resumed work, adding the hands and the crucial rope (the "berceuse" element) that transformed a straightforward portrait into the symbolic composition. He mentioned the resumption of work four times in a single week — in letters to Gauguin, Theo, Arnold Koning, and Theo again.

Van Gogh painted five versions total: this Met version, plus versions now in Boston (MFA), Chicago (Art Institute), Amsterdam (Stedelijk Museum), and Otterlo (Kröller-Müller Museum). The Met version is the one Augustine Roulin chose for herself — Van Gogh noted approvingly that "she had a good eye and took the best." He intended a version for each of the people most important to him: Theo, Gauguin, Bernard, and the Roulin family.

The title "La Berceuse" means both "lullaby" and "woman who rocks the cradle." The concept was inspired by Pierre Loti's novel Pêcheur d'Islande (Icelandic Fisherman) and conversations with Gauguin about the isolation of Icelandic fishermen. Van Gogh imagined the painting hung in a fishing boat's cabin, where sailors "would experience a feeling of being rocked, reminding them of their own lullabies." He envisioned La Berceuse as the central panel of a triptych, flanked by two Sunflowers paintings "like candelabra" — an explicit allusion to the traditional religious triptych format.

## Artistic Intent

- **Sing "a lullaby in colour."** Van Gogh's central ambition was to create a painting whose colour harmonies would function like music — soothing, comforting, and emotionally transporting, without requiring narrative or symbolic decoding.
- **Create a secular Madonna.** The triptych format, the centred frontal figure, and the decorative "Cloth of Honor" background all deliberately evoke religious art, with Augustine Roulin replacing the Virgin Mary as a modern embodiment of maternal consolation.
- **Console the lonely and suffering.** The painting's intended audience — isolated fishermen, "at once children and martyrs" — reveals Van Gogh's social vision: art as comfort for ordinary people enduring hardship.
- **Achieve a deliberately primitive style.** The simplified, "chromo"-like treatment was not a failure of sophistication but a conscious strategy to make the painting emotionally accessible to its intended popular audience.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Triptych concept.** Van Gogh's plan to flank La Berceuse with Sunflowers — documented in a letter sketch — represents one of the most ambitious ensemble conceptions in his oeuvre, fusing religious art tradition with modern secular subject matter. This triptych was displayed as Van Gogh intended at the National Gallery in London in 2024.
- **Five-version series.** The existence of five autograph versions, painted using a tracing technique to transfer the composition, makes La Berceuse a key case study in Van Gogh's repetitions practice and his belief that important images deserved multiple iterations.
- **Post-breakdown creation.** The painting's genesis — begun before and completed after the ear incident — makes it an extraordinary document of artistic determination in the face of personal crisis. The addition of the rope and the conceptual transformation of a portrait into "La Berceuse" occurred in the immediate aftermath of the breakdown.
- **Synesthesia of art and music.** Van Gogh's explicit equation of colour with lullaby — and his references to Berlioz and Wagner alongside the colour scheme — represents one of the most articulate statements of the Symbolist ideal of correspondence between the arts.
