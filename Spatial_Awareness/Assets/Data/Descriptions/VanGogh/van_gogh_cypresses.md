# Cypresses

## Metadata

- **Title:** Cypresses
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (late June)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 93.4 × 74 cm (36 3/4 × 29 1/8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F613 / JH1746
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Rogers Fund, 1949
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape

## Subject

A close-up, vertical view of two tall cypress trees dominating the composition. The dark, flame-like forms of the cypresses rise from the lower portion of the canvas to nearly the top, their dense foliage rendered as swirling, undulating masses of deep green. To the left and right, the surrounding Provençal landscape is visible — rolling hills, smaller trees, and scrubby vegetation. Above, a turbulent sky of deep blue swirls with lighter patches and a crescent moon and star visible in the upper portion. Low-growing roses or flowers appear at the base of the trees.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Dominated by the deep, near-black greens of the cypress foliage, set against a sky of intense blues with turquoise and lighter blue accents. The surrounding vegetation introduces warmer greens, ochres, and touches of pink from flowers at the base. The overall tonality is cool and deep.
- **Brushwork:** Among Van Gogh's most intensely worked surfaces. The cypress foliage is rendered with thick, rhythmic, swirling strokes that create a powerful sense of organic movement — as if the trees are alive with internal energy. The sky echoes this turbulence with its own curving, rolling strokes. The impasto is extremely heavy throughout.
- **Composition:** Vertical format, unusual for a landscape. The cypress trees fill the canvas from bottom to near-top, creating a powerful upward thrust. This is a close-up, almost portrait-like treatment of the trees, granting them individual presence and monumental status.
- **Atmosphere:** Intense, dramatic, and somewhat ominous. The swirling sky and flame-like tree forms create a sense of cosmic energy and restless vitality.

## Context

Cypresses was painted in late June 1889, during the first weeks of Van Gogh's voluntary stay at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. After demonstrating stability, he had received permission to paint outside the asylum's walled garden. He immediately devoted himself to the cypresses that dotted the Provençal countryside, describing them as "beautiful as regards lines and proportions, like an Egyptian obelisk."

Van Gogh envisioned the cypress as a subject that could rival his sunflower paintings in expressive power. He wrote to Theo that the cypresses constantly occupied his thoughts, wanting to "make something of them like my canvases of sunflowers." He sent a reed-pen drawing of this composition to Theo (now in the Brooklyn Museum) to show what the paintings looked like.

The painting was exhibited at the 1890 Salon des Indépendants in Paris, where it was shown publicly during Van Gogh's lifetime. It was acquired by the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1949 through the Rogers Fund. One of only two close-up, vertical views of cypresses Van Gogh produced, it represents his most concentrated study of the motif.

## Artistic Intent

- **Make the cypress a protagonist.** Rather than using the tree as a landscape element, Van Gogh made it the sole subject — a portrait of a tree treated with the same intensity and attention as a human portrait.
- **Challenge the association of cypresses with death.** While traditionally funereal symbols in Mediterranean culture, Van Gogh reinterpreted cypresses as symbols of consolation, resilience, and aspiration — bridges between earth and sky.
- **Create a pendant to the sunflower series.** Van Gogh explicitly wanted his cypress paintings to achieve the same status as his celebrated sunflower paintings, suggesting he saw the dark vertical tree as the complementary opposite of the bright circular flower.
- **Capture the flame-like quality of the tree.** Van Gogh's brushwork deliberately evokes fire, transforming the static tree into a dynamic, living form that seems to burn with energy.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Vertical landscape format.** The portrait-orientation landscape is unusual in Van Gogh's work and in landscape painting generally. The format forces an intimate, confrontational relationship between viewer and tree.
- **Maximum impasto.** The paint surface of Cypresses is among the most heavily loaded of Van Gogh's career. The three-dimensional texture of the paint creates actual shadows and highlights on the canvas surface.
- **Relationship to The Starry Night.** Painted around the same time, Cypresses shares the swirling brushwork and cosmic energy of The Starry Night. The cypresses in both paintings serve as intermediaries between the earthly and celestial realms.
- **Recent scholarship (2023 Met exhibition).** The Met's 2023 exhibition "Van Gogh's Cypresses" reinterpreted the cypress motif as symbolizing consolation and resilience rather than morbidity, drawing on Van Gogh's letters and the paintings' context to portray the trees as sources of imaginative freedom and hope during adversity.
