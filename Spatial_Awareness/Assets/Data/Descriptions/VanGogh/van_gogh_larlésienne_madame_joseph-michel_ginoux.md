# L'Arlésienne: Madame Joseph-Michel Ginoux

## Metadata

- **Title:** L'Arlésienne: Madame Joseph-Michel Ginoux (née Marie Julien, 1848–1911)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1888–1889
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 91.4 × 73.7 cm (36 × 29 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F488 / JH1624
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Bequest of Sam A. Lewisohn, 1951
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Portrait

## Subject

A half-length seated portrait of Marie Ginoux, the proprietress of the Café de la Gare in Arles, wearing the traditional dark costume of an Arlésienne (woman of Arles). She is seated at a green table on which several books are placed. Her dark hair is parted in the center. She wears a black dress with a dark shawl or fichu. Her expression is calm, composed, and contemplative. The background is a flat, warm yellow. Her skin is rendered in pale, almost colorless tones.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** High contrast between the black dress, bright yellow background, green table, and the muted flesh tones. The coloring is deliberately simplified into flat areas of bold color, with minimal modeling.
- **Brushwork:** More fluid and less impasto-heavy than many of Van Gogh's Arles works. The background is applied in broad, even strokes. The figure is outlined with clear contours, showing Japanese print influence.
- **Composition:** Central seated figure, viewed slightly from the side, leaning on the table. The books on the table are a distinguishing feature of this particular version (the first Musée d'Orsay version has gloves and an umbrella instead).
- **Expression:** Calm, dignified, and introspective. Van Gogh described the portrait as having colorless, matt flesh tones, calm and simple eyes.

## Context

Marie Ginoux and her husband Joseph-Michel Ginoux ran the Café de la Gare at 30 Place Lamartine, where Van Gogh lodged from May to mid-September 1888 before moving to the Yellow House. The portrait emerged from a joint session with Gauguin in early November 1888: while Gauguin made a charcoal drawing of Madame Ginoux, Van Gogh painted her directly onto canvas at high speed. He described the first version as having been "knocked off in one hour."

This Metropolitan Museum version is the second painting, in which Van Gogh replaced the gloves and umbrella of the first version with books on the table. Van Gogh later painted five additional portraits of Madame Ginoux at the Saint-Rémy asylum in February 1890, working from Gauguin's charcoal drawing rather than from life. Of these later versions, one was intended for Gauguin, one for Theo, one for himself, and one for Madame Ginoux.

Van Gogh articulated his philosophy of portraiture in a letter to his sister Wil: he sought to create portraits that would appear as revelations to people a hundred years hence, using modern knowledge of color to render and exalt character, not photographic likeness.

## Artistic Intent

- **Create a portrait that reveals character through color.** Van Gogh explicitly rejected photographic likeness in favor of using color symbolically to express the sitter's personality and spirit.
- **Document the Arlésienne type.** The dark-haired women of Arles had a legendary reputation, and Van Gogh's portrait captures this cultural identity through the traditional costume and dignified bearing.
- **Demonstrate rapid, confident execution.** The first version was completed in a single hour, reflecting Van Gogh's aspiration to paint with spontaneous mastery.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Collaboration with Gauguin.** The L'Arlésienne paintings are a direct product of the Van Gogh-Gauguin collaboration in Arles, emerging from the same sitting where both artists worked simultaneously on the same subject.
- **Serial portraiture.** The six total versions make this Van Gogh's most extensively repeated portrait subject, offering a rare opportunity to study how he varied and refined a single composition over time and across different working conditions.
- **Japanese-influenced flatness.** The bold outlines, flat color areas, and simplified modeling reflect Van Gogh's continued engagement with Japanese aesthetic principles.
