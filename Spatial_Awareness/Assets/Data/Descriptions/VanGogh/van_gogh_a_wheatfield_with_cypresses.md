# A Wheatfield, with Cypresses

## Metadata

- **Title:** A Wheatfield, with Cypresses (also known as A Cornfield, with Cypresses)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (September)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 72.1 × 90.9 cm (28.4 × 35.8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F615 / JH1756
- **Location:** The National Gallery, London, UK
- **Credit line:** Bought, Courtauld Fund, 1923
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape

## Subject

A golden wheat field occupies the foreground, rippling in the wind. In the middle ground, two tall cypress trees command the right side of the composition, their dark forms rising majestically toward the sky. To the left, a stout, rounded olive bush provides a visual counterpoint. Beyond the field, the Alpilles mountains form a hard ridge along the horizon. Above, a turbulent sky of blue and white — clouds swirling and rolling with energy that echoes the movement of the wheat below. Scattered poppies and wildflowers dot the edges of the field. The scene is a pure landscape with no human figures or animals.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Rich warm yellows and golds in the wheat field, contrasting with the deep greens of the cypress trees and the blue-greens of the olive bush. The sky is a complex mixture of blue, white, and touches of lilac. The Alpilles mountains echo the sky's blues. Touches of red from poppies provide small accents of warmth. The overall colour effect is one of summer abundance under a dramatic sky.
- **Brushwork:** Vigorous and varied. The wheat uses repetitive vertical strokes for stalks with diagonal marks for the feathery heads, creating a layered texture that suggests swaying movement. The cypress trees are painted in thick, dark, upward-sweeping marks with feathered edges. The olive bush uses multi-directional blue-green strokes creating a maze-like texture. The clouds are built up with swirling, overlapping marks of white and blue. The paint application throughout is thinner and more controlled than in the plein-air Met original, reflecting the studio conditions.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format. The wheat field provides a warm, golden base. The cypress trees and olive bush create strong vertical and rounded forms in the middle ground — visual foils of each other in almost every quality (tall/short, dark/light, vertical/rounded, mono-directional/multi-directional strokes). The mountains and sky fill the upper portion with horizontal and swirling movement.
- **Surface condition:** The painting is unlined and was never varnished or waxed, preserving an exceptionally original surface condition that reveals Van Gogh's technique with unusual clarity.

## Context

This painting is a studio repetition of the plein-air original now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art (F717, 73.2 × 93.4 cm). Van Gogh painted the Met version directly from nature in early July 1889 and regarded it as one of his "best" summer landscapes, describing it to Theo: "a canvas of cypresses with some ears of wheat, some poppies, a blue sky like a piece of Scotch plaid; the former painted with a thick impasto like the Monticelli's, and the wheat field in the sun, which represents the extreme heat, very thick too."

After a severe mental health episode in late July and August, Van Gogh resumed painting in late August/early September. He first made a reed-pen drawing of the composition (now at the Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam), then copied it twice in oils: this near-identical studio version (F615) and a smaller version (F743, now in a private collection). The National Gallery version was probably painted in a single sitting with charcoal underdrawing visible beneath the paint. Van Gogh used zinc white (zinc oxide) for the clouds and cobalt blue for the sky.

Both the Met original and this studio version were sent to Theo in Paris in September 1889. The National Gallery acquired the painting in 1923 using the Courtauld Fund — one of the earliest acquisitions of a Van Gogh by a major British institution.

## Artistic Intent

- **Refine and consolidate an important plein-air study.** Following his established "repetitions" practice, Van Gogh created the studio version to achieve a more considered, balanced interpretation of a composition he valued highly.
- **Express "extreme heat" through painting.** Van Gogh's description emphasises the sensory qualities he sought to convey — the shimmering intensity of a Provençal summer, the weight and presence of the cypresses, the dynamic energy of the wind.
- **Elevate the cypress as a subject.** Van Gogh repeatedly expressed fascination with cypress trees, comparing them to "Egyptian obelisks" and lamenting that no one had painted them as he saw them. This composition was central to his campaign to establish the cypress as a worthy subject.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Key repetitions case study.** The existence of the plein-air original (Met), the reed-pen drawing (Van Gogh Museum), and two studio oil versions makes this one of the most fully documented sequences in Van Gogh's repetitions practice, showing how he progressed from observation through drawing to refined studio painting.
- **Diurnal counterpart to The Starry Night.** The composition — with its towering cypresses, rolling Alpilles mountains, and turbulent sky — provides a daylight complement to the nocturnal Starry Night, sharing compositional elements (cypress, mountains, swirling sky) but expressed in the heat and light of summer.
- **Exceptional surface preservation.** The unlined, unvarnished condition of the National Gallery painting provides art historians and conservators with a rare opportunity to study Van Gogh's original paint surface without later interventions.
- **Studio versus plein air.** Comparing this version with the Met original reveals Van Gogh's studio practice: thinner paint, more controlled application, charcoal underdrawing, but with the same expressive intensity maintained through compositional fidelity and energetic brushwork.
