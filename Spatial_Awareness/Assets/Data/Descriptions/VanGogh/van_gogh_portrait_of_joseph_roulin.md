# Portrait of Joseph Roulin

## Metadata

- **Title:** Portrait of Joseph Roulin
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** Early 1889
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 64.4 × 55.2 cm (25 3/8 × 21 3/4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F439 / JH1675
- **Location:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Gift of Mr. and Mrs. William A. M. Burden, Mr. and Mrs. Paul Rosenberg, Nelson A. Rockefeller, Mr. and Mrs. Armand P. Bartos, The Sidney and Harriet Janis Collection, Mr. and Mrs. William S. Paley, and an anonymous donor (by exchange)
- **Period:** Arles period (1888–1889)
- **Genre:** Portrait

## Subject

A half-length portrait of Joseph-Étienne Roulin, a postal employee at the station in Arles, shown seated against a richly decorated floral background. Roulin wears his blue postal uniform with gold trim and buttons, and his distinctive blue cap bearing the word "POSTES." He has a large, full brown beard with stylized curls, a ruddy face, and a direct, steady gaze. The background is filled with a lush wallpaper-like pattern of flowers — poppies, cornflowers, daisies, and roses — in vivid colors.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** High-key and saturated. The blue uniform contrasts boldly with the warm floral background of greens, pinks, reds, and yellows. Roulin's ruddy, flushed complexion — attributed by Van Gogh to heavy drinking — adds warm accents. The gold trim on the uniform provides metallic highlights.
- **Brushwork:** Vigorous and varied. The beard is rendered with stylized, rhythmic curls in thick impasto. The face is painted with quick, fluent strokes showing less concern for precise modeling than for capturing character. The floral background is painted with greater precision and detail than the face, creating an unusual reversal of typical portrait hierarchy.
- **Composition:** Bust-length, frontal-to-slight-three-quarter view. Roulin fills the frame, his solid physical presence dominating the picture plane. The decorative floral background wraps around him in an almost medallion-like arrangement, echoing Japanese print conventions.
- **Expression:** Warm, solid, unpretentious. The face conveys a quality of reliability, humour, and paternal warmth that Van Gogh deeply valued in his friend.

## Context

Joseph Roulin worked as a postman at the train station in Arles, where Van Gogh frequently went to send paintings to his brother Theo. They became close friends, bonded in part by shared leftist political sympathies. Van Gogh described Roulin in a letter to Theo as "a man who is not bitter, not melancholy, not perfect, not happy and also not always perfectly honest. But such a good fellow, so wise, so feeling and so faithful."

Between August 1888 and April 1889, Van Gogh painted six consecutive portraits of Joseph Roulin alone, plus over twenty portraits of the entire Roulin family. This MoMA version, the last of the six Joseph portraits, was painted in early 1889 — after the ear incident and during a period when Roulin had been transferred to Marseilles but returned to visit Van Gogh. Three of the six Joseph portraits feature floral backgrounds; the MoMA version uses summer flowers.

Roulin proved a crucial support during Van Gogh's crisis. After the ear mutilation in December 1888, Roulin tended to Van Gogh, visited him in hospital, wrote to his family to reassure them, and provided consistent emotional support during the difficult months that followed. Van Gogh saw in Roulin a Socratic everyman figure and a surrogate "big brother."

MoMA acquired this painting in 1989 from a private collection in Zurich for $58 million.

## Artistic Intent

- **Portray character through color and decoration.** Van Gogh wanted his portraits to be "revelations" that used color expressively to convey personality. The warm, abundant flowers surrounding Roulin suggest his generous, life-affirming nature.
- **Honour a loyal friend.** The Roulin family portrait series was Van Gogh's most sustained portraiture project, born from genuine affection and gratitude.
- **Reference Japanese prints.** The flat, decorative background of flowers recalls Japanese print compositions that Van Gogh admired. The floral patterning elevates a working-class postal worker to an almost iconic status.
- **Paint without preparatory sketches.** Van Gogh worked directly on the canvas, without preliminary drawing, applying paint with spontaneous confidence.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Culmination of the Roulin series.** As the final portrait of Joseph, this painting represents Van Gogh's most fully realized treatment of his friend, synthesizing months of observation and deepening friendship into a single, definitive image.
- **Decorative-portrait fusion.** The rich floral background makes this portrait a bridge between traditional portraiture and the decorative, Symbolist-inflected approach that would influence Bonnard, Vuillard, and the Nabis.
- **Working-class subject as icon.** Van Gogh's elevation of a modest postal worker to the status of a monumental painted figure challenged the conventions of who deserved to be portrayed with such dignity and chromatic richness.
