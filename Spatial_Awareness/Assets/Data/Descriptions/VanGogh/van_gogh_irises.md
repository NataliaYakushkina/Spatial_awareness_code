# Irises

## Metadata

- **Title:** Irises
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (May)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 74.3 × 94.3 cm (29 1/4 × 37 1/8 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F608 / JH1691
- **Location:** J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles, USA
- **Accession number:** 90.PA.20
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Garden scene / Floral landscape

## Subject

A dense bed of blooming irises seen from close range, filling the entire composition. The flowers are predominantly deep blue-violet, their petals curving and twisting in varied silhouettes. Among them, a single white iris stands out conspicuously. Below the flowers, lush green sword-like leaves create a rhythmic counterpoint. In the background, orange-yellow marigolds and other warm-toned flowers form a band of contrasting colour. The reddish-brown earth is visible between the plant stems. The composition is cropped, with irises overflowing the canvas edges on all sides, creating a sense of immersion.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Built on complementary contrasts. The deep blue-violet irises are set against the red-orange earth, and the green leaves contrast with the warm background flowers. The lone white iris provides a focal pause amid the saturated colours. Research by Getty conservators has revealed that the irises were originally more violet — a mixture of geranium lake red and blue pigments — but the red component has faded over time, shifting the flowers toward pure blue.
- **Brushwork:** Varied and descriptive. Each iris petal is individually modeled with curving strokes that follow its form, creating an endless variety of curved silhouettes. The leaves use long, parallel strokes. The earth is hatched with short, directional marks. The background flowers use smaller, more uniform dabs.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format with an extremely cropped, close-up viewpoint. The flowers overflow the canvas borders, creating a sense that the viewer is immersed within the flower bed rather than observing it from a distance. The composition is divided into broad horizontal bands of colour: brown earth, green leaves, blue-violet flowers, and yellow-orange background.
- **Japanese influence:** The cropped composition, broad areas of flat colour, sharp diagonal elements, and focus on foreground detail are directly influenced by Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints. Van Gogh associated irises with Japan, where the native species were highly prized in art.

## Context

Irises was painted within the first week of Van Gogh's arrival at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in Saint-Rémy in May 1889. During this initial period, he was confined to the hospital grounds and its walled garden, which became his primary subject. He worked from nature directly in the garden, and a pine pollen cone embedded in the paint surface provides physical evidence of his plein-air working process.

Van Gogh referred to the painting in a letter to Theo as a study of "violet irises." He considered it a study rather than a finished composition, and there are no known preparatory drawings for it. Despite this modest self-assessment, his brother Theo submitted Irises and Starry Night Over the Rhône to the 1889 Salon des Indépendants in Paris, where they made what Theo described as "a very good impression." This was one of the few exhibitions of Van Gogh's work during his lifetime.

The painting's ownership history is notable. It was first owned by French art critic Octave Mirbeau, one of Van Gogh's earliest supporters, who praised the artist's understanding of "the exquisite nature of flowers." In November 1987, the painting was sold at Sotheby's for $53.9 million, at the time the most expensive painting ever sold at auction. The J. Paul Getty Museum acquired it in 1990 when the buyer could not secure financing.

## Artistic Intent

- **Find therapeutic purpose in painting.** Van Gogh was optimistic about the restorative effects of painting in the hospital garden, writing that he believed all his "faculties for work will come back quite quickly." Irises was among the first products of this therapeutic engagement with nature.
- **Individualize each flower.** Unlike Impressionist flower pieces where plants dissolve into spots of colour, Van Gogh carefully studied each iris's movements and shapes, creating distinct, individualized forms — treating flowers with the same observational intensity as portraits.
- **Exploit complementary colour contrasts.** The painting is structured around the play of complementaries: blue-violet against orange-yellow, green against red-brown. Van Gogh was fascinated by how colours appear brighter and more vivid when paired with their chromatic opposite.
- **Engage with Japanese aesthetics.** Van Gogh combined his love of Japanese prints with his direct experience of Provençal nature. For him, both Japan and Provence represented exotic places of stronger sun, clearer skies, and brighter colours.

## Stylistic Significance

- **First major work at Saint-Rémy.** Irises inaugurated Van Gogh's extraordinarily productive asylum period (approximately 130 paintings in twelve months) and set the tone for the intense, nature-focused work that followed.
- **Colour degradation as art-historical evidence.** Getty conservation research has demonstrated that the flowers were originally more violet than the blue we see today. The geranium lake red pigment mixed with blue has faded, fundamentally altering the painting's colour relationships. This discovery illuminates the gap between Van Gogh's intended effects and the painting's current appearance.
- **The lone white iris.** The single white flower amid the blue-violet mass has invited extensive interpretation — as a symbol of Van Gogh's isolation, a pictorial device for visual contrast, or a spiritual emblem. Its significance remains debated.
- **Japanese-Western synthesis.** Irises is one of the most successful examples of Van Gogh's lifelong project to merge Japanese compositional principles with Western oil painting technique and Post-Impressionist colour theory.
- **Auction record.** The 1987 sale price of $53.9 million made Irises briefly the most expensive painting ever sold, a milestone in the escalation of art market prices that marked the late twentieth century.
