# The Olive Trees

## Metadata

- **Title:** The Olive Trees (Olive Trees in a Mountainous Landscape)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1889 (June–July)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 72.6 × 91.4 cm (28 5/8 × 36 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F712 / JH1740
- **Location:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Mrs. John Hay Whitney Bequest, 581.1998
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Landscape

## Subject

A group of gnarled olive trees in the foreground and middle ground, their twisted trunks and silvery-green foliage filling much of the canvas. The ground beneath is scored with energetic, undulating lines suggesting rough terrain. Behind the trees, rolling hills rise toward the Alpilles mountains, which merge with a light-washed sky containing a single large bundled, cloud-like form. The landscape pulsates with energy; nothing is still.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Greens dominate — from deep olive greens to lighter silver-greens in the foliage — set against earth tones of ochre and sienna in the ground. The mountains transition from green to blue-violet. The sky is pale blue-white with the distinctive cloud formation. The overall tonality suggests intense Mediterranean afternoon light.
- **Brushwork:** Among Van Gogh's most rhythmically intense. Every surface is animated with swirling, curving strokes. The olive tree foliage uses circular, spiraling marks. The ground is scored with parallel undulating lines suggesting invisible torrents of energy. The mountains and sky continue the same rhythmic movement. The entire composition shares a unified visual rhythm.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format with olive trees occupying the foreground and middle ground. The mountains rise in rolling forms behind, and the sky contains the prominent ectoplasmic cloud. The composition is layered but unified by the consistent rhythmic brushwork that runs through every element.
- **Atmosphere:** Blazing, energetic, almost hallucinatory. The intensity of the brushwork and the unified rhythmic movement create a landscape that seems to vibrate with internal life force.

## Context

Van Gogh painted this work in June–July 1889, early in his stay at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. After writing to Theo, "I did a landscape with olive trees and also a new study of a starry sky," he later sent both paintings to Paris and noted: "The olive trees with the white cloud and the mountains behind, as well as the rise of the moon and the night effect, are exaggerations from the point of view of the general arrangement; the outlines are accentuated as in some old woodcuts."

Van Gogh explicitly conceived this painting as the daylight complement to The Starry Night. Both share the same rhythmic, swirling brushwork and expressive exaggeration, but one captures the intensity of Mediterranean daytime and the other the mystery of the night sky. Together they represent Van Gogh's principle of going beyond "the photographic and silly perfection of some painters" to achieve an intensity born of colour and linear rhythms.

This is one of approximately 15 olive tree paintings Van Gogh produced at Saint-Rémy. The olive groves held both visual and spiritual significance for him — their gnarled forms, changing colours, and association with the Garden of Gethsemane made them ideal subjects for his synthesis of observation and emotion.

## Artistic Intent

- **Create a daytime complement to The Starry Night.** Van Gogh explicitly paired the two paintings, one showing diurnal intensity and the other nocturnal mystery, using similar expressive techniques applied to contrasting conditions.
- **Go beyond photographic accuracy.** Van Gogh acknowledged the "exaggerations" in the painting — the accentuated outlines, the heightened rhythms — as deliberate choices to convey emotional and spiritual truth rather than literal appearances.
- **Express the spiritual energy of nature.** The unified rhythmic movement — shared by trees, ground, mountains, and sky — conveys Van Gogh's pantheistic sense that all of nature is animated by a single vital force.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Companion to The Starry Night.** The painting's status as the explicit daylight counterpart to one of the most famous paintings in Western art gives it exceptional art-historical importance. Both are in MoMA's collection.
- **Unified rhythmic brushwork.** The consistency of the swirling, curving marks across every element — trees, ground, mountains, sky — creates one of the most visually unified compositions in Van Gogh's oeuvre, approaching near-abstraction in its emphasis on pattern and rhythm over description.
- **Woodcut-like exaggeration.** Van Gogh's comparison of his outlines to "old woodcuts" signals his conscious engagement with earlier graphic traditions and his interest in strong linear expression alongside colour.
- **Olive tree series.** As one of the earliest and most energetically painted of the series, this work establishes the visual vocabulary Van Gogh would refine over the following months.
