# Self-Portrait (Rijksmuseum)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Self-Portrait (Zelfportret)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1887
- **Medium:** Oil on cardboard
- **Dimensions:** 42 × 34 cm (16.5 × 13.4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F295
- **Location:** Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, Netherlands
- **Provenance:** Given by the artist to Andries Bonger (Theo van Gogh's brother-in-law); donated by Bonger's widow to the Rijksmuseum in 1936
- **Period:** Paris period (1886–1888)
- **Genre:** Self-portrait

## Subject

A bust-length self-portrait of Van Gogh wearing a brown jacket and a gray felt hat. He is depicted as a fashionably dressed Parisian man, clean and presentable, gazing directly at the viewer. The portrait conveys a confident, self-possessed demeanor — markedly different from the rough, peasant-associated self-image of some other self-portraits.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Striking use of color contrasts. The hat is rendered in grays and blue-greens. The jacket is brown with warm undertones. The face uses warm flesh tones contrasted with green and blue shadows. The beard is distinctly orange-red. The background mixes blue, green, and neutral tones applied in short rhythmic strokes.
- **Brushwork:** Rhythmic, directional strokes of distinct colors laid side by side, showing the influence of Neo-Impressionism. The background strokes radiate or flow in organized patterns. The face and clothing are modeled with more blended, slightly broader strokes, but still visibly structured.
- **Support:** Painted on cardboard rather than canvas — a cheaper alternative Van Gogh used for economy. The cardboard ground contributes to a slightly different surface texture and absorption quality compared to his canvas paintings.
- **Color degradation:** Research has revealed that a red pigment in the face has faded over time. The face was originally more colorful and warmly toned than its current appearance suggests.
- **Expression:** The eyes are prominently rendered — green with careful attention to the gaze direction. The overall expression is calm, alert, and self-aware.

## Context

Van Gogh moved to Paris in March 1886 to join his brother Theo, who was working as an art dealer. Encountering the colorful, light-filled paintings of the Impressionists and Neo-Impressionists was a revelation. He immediately began experimenting with the new French style, using self-portraits as his primary vehicle because he could not afford models.

This particular self-portrait was given by Van Gogh to Andries Bonger, the brother of Theo's wife Jo van Gogh-Bonger. This gifting pattern was typical — Van Gogh frequently gave paintings to friends and family. It entered the Rijksmuseum collection in 1936 through donation by Bonger's widow.

## Artistic Intent

- **Try out the new French painting style.** The Rijksmuseum describes this painting as Van Gogh's attempt to test the colorful Parisian approach on himself — using rhythmic brushstrokes and striking color contrasts influenced by Impressionism and Neo-Impressionism.
- **Present himself as a Parisian.** Unlike his working-class straw hat portraits, this painting shows Van Gogh as a fashionably dressed city dweller, suggesting his desire to belong to the Parisian art world.
- **Practice and economy.** Self-portraiture on cheap cardboard allowed Van Gogh to develop his technique without the expense of canvas or models.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Paris transformation documented.** This painting is a clear visual record of Van Gogh's stylistic transition — the color and brushwork are dramatically different from anything he produced in the Netherlands just months earlier.
- **Modest scale and materials.** The use of cardboard and relatively small dimensions place this among Van Gogh's more intimate, study-like self-portraits rather than his ambitious exhibition pieces.
- **Rijksmuseum context.** As one of the few Van Gogh paintings in the Rijksmuseum (most are held by the adjacent Van Gogh Museum), this self-portrait provides visitors with an important point of encounter with his work in the broader context of Dutch art history.
