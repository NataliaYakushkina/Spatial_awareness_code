# Irises (Still Life: Vase with Irises Against a Pink Background)

## Metadata

- **Title:** Irises (Still Life: Vase with Irises)
- **Artist:** Vincent van Gogh
- **Year:** 1890 (May)
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas
- **Dimensions:** 73.7 × 92.1 cm (29 × 36 1/4 in)
- **Catalogue raisonné:** F680 / JH1978
- **Location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, USA
- **Credit line:** Gift of Adele R. Levy, 1958
- **Period:** Saint-Rémy period (1889–1890)
- **Genre:** Still life

## Subject

A large bouquet of irises arranged in a vase, set on a table against a pink background. The irises are depicted in rich violet-blue tones (originally more purple, before pigment fading) with green stems and leaves cascading over the vase's rim. The vase is placed slightly off-centre. A horizontal line establishes the tabletop. The composition is dense with blooms — some fully open, some drooping — filling the canvas with organic forms.

## Visual Description

- **Palette:** Originally structured around the complementary contrast of violet flowers against a pink background — a "harmonious and soft" combination of greens, pinks, and violets. However, the fugitive red pigments Van Gogh used have faded significantly over time. The flowers have shifted from purple to blue, and the pink background has whitened considerably. The current appearance does not accurately represent Van Gogh's intended colour scheme. Traces of pink along the tabletop and in rose petals remain as faint reminders of the original tonality.
- **Brushwork:** Confident, fluid, and controlled. Van Gogh described working with "calm and steady enthusiasm" and brushstrokes that "come to me and follow each other very logically." Each iris is individually shaped with curving, descriptive strokes. The background and table are more evenly painted, allowing the floral forms to stand out.
- **Composition:** Horizontal format. The vase is placed slightly off-centre with irises spilling outward in a naturalistic arrangement. A shared horizon line connects this painting to its three companion pieces (the upright Irises, and two Roses canvases), designed to function together as a decorative ensemble.
- **Scale:** The flowers are monumental — life-size or larger — filling the canvas with generous, exuberant forms.

## Context

In May 1890, during his last days at the Saint-Rémy asylum and just before his departure for Auvers-sur-Oise, Van Gogh painted four ambitious still lifes of spring flowers: two of irises and two of roses. These were the only still lifes of any ambition he undertook during his entire yearlong stay. He conceived them as a decorative ensemble comparable to the Sunflower series painted in Arles.

Van Gogh cut a roll of canvas into four equal rectangles of corresponding size. He anchored each composition with a slightly off-centre vase and unified them with a common horizon line. He then structured the series around two pairs of complementary colours: yellow and violet for one pair, and pink and green for the other — varied across vertical and horizontal formats. The Met's horizontal Irises (violet against pink, "harmonious and soft") was paired with the Van Gogh Museum's vertical Irises (violet against "startling citron yellow," "tremendously disparate complementaries"). Similarly, two Roses compositions formed a complementary pair.

Van Gogh described the series in a letter to Theo: "I am doing a canvas of roses with a light green background and two canvases representing big bunches of violet irises, one lot against a pink background in which the effect is soft and harmonious because of the combination of greens, pinks, violets. On the other hand, the other violet bunch . . . stands out against a startling citron background . . . so it is an effect of tremendously disparate complementaries, which strengthen each other by their juxtaposition."

He checked himself out of the asylum on May 16, 1890, calling the recent days' work "a revelation of colour." The paintings were left behind to dry and shipped to him in Auvers in late June, arriving about a month before his death. Both Met paintings (Irises and Roses) were owned by the artist's mother until her death in 1907.

The Met reunited all four paintings for the first time since the artist's death in the 2015 exhibition "Van Gogh: Irises and Roses."

## Artistic Intent

- **Create a decorative ensemble to rival the Sunflowers.** Van Gogh explicitly conceived the four flower paintings as a unified group, paralleling the Arles Sunflower series in ambition and decorative purpose.
- **Explore complementary colour contrasts systematically.** Each of the four canvases was designed to demonstrate a different complementary colour relationship — some harmonious, some intensely contrasting — making the series a comprehensive study in colour theory.
- **Achieve "calm, unremitting ardor."** Van Gogh's own phrase for the spirit he wanted in his final Saint-Rémy work — passionate but controlled, intense but serene.
- **Provide a valedictory statement.** As the last ambitious paintings before leaving the asylum, these still lifes represent a conscious summing-up of Van Gogh's development as a colourist.

## Stylistic Significance

- **Four-painting ensemble.** The series is unique in Van Gogh's oeuvre for its systematic, pre-planned approach to complementary colour contrasts across multiple canvases of uniform size and linked composition.
- **Pigment fading as art-historical evidence.** Like the Getty Irises, this painting has undergone significant colour change due to the fading of fugitive red pigments. The Met's 2015 exhibition included digital reconstructions showing the original intended colours alongside the current appearance, providing one of the most detailed studies of pigment degradation in Van Gogh's work.
- **Bridge between Sunflowers and final work.** Van Gogh conceived these still lifes with explicit reference to his Arles Sunflowers, making them a direct link between his two greatest periods of production.
- **Last ambitious Saint-Rémy paintings.** Completed in the final days before departure, these represent the culmination of Van Gogh's asylum-period engagement with colour theory and decorative painting.
